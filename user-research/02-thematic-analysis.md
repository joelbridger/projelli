# 02 — Thematic analysis

*Lead synthesis. Method: the lead read both transcripts fully and wrote independent theme
candidates BEFORE reading any lane output (`analysis-drafts/lead-theme-memo.md`); three
analysis lanes (evidence coding, workflow/JTBD, trust/adoption/counteranalysis) worked the
transcripts in parallel; themes below are the reconciliation, tested against the complete
129-item evidence ledger (01). Every theme cites ledger IDs (E-###) and can be traced to
timestamped transcript passages through them.*

## How codes became themes

The ledger produced 193 grounded codes. Consolidation steps:
1. Codes with ≥4 occurrences (30 codes) were clustered by what job or fear they describe.
2. Clusters were tested against the FULL session (both parts) for counter-evidence — the
   ledger's negative-evidence index (01 §b) was applied to every cluster, and the skeptic
   lane's interviewer-influence audit downgraded any cluster built mostly on pitch-reactions.
3. Two of the lead's original candidate themes were **demoted** in this process (recorded at
   the bottom) — one for provenance (interviewer-introduced), one for thinness.
4. The surviving seven themes each have: central idea, boundaries (in/out), supporting and
   contradicting evidence, interpretation, product significance, confidence.

## T1 — "What's the latest and greatest?" Source-of-truth reconciliation is the center of gravity

**Central idea.** The firm's information about any client fact is scattered across OneDrive
files, email threads, Wealthbox notes/tasks, Jump transcripts, PowerPoint decks, Schwab, and
people's memories — and the daily, unsolved question is *which value is current and where's
the proof*. She wants to ask one question and get a short, **dated**, **cited** answer that
reconciles the sources and flags disagreement.

**Boundaries.** IN: cross-source recall, recency, conflict surfacing, email searchability,
"scavenger hunt" elimination, Philip's source-of-truth complaint, right-sized timeline
answers. OUT: proactive/standing reports (T6), the artifact question of a maintained "map"
(demoted — see bottom).

**Supporting evidence.** The umbrella-policy scenario — her own, stated as a live problem,
with her own answer spec: "while the file shows one million dollar, we've received email
updates that say two million dollar, and these are the dates" (E-066, E-067; actual-behavior
+ high confidence). The HSA/company-benefits variant (E-069). Philip's recurring "what is the
source of truth on XYZ... is it a scavenger hunt in Wealthbox" (E-104). Wealthbox search
failing on wording ("401k" vs "retirement account at JP Morgan", E-019) and email being
unsearchable at all (E-020, E-088). Her closing, direct answer to "where are the needs?":
source-of-truth inquiries with AI summaries "would be hugely valuable for our firm" (E-128).
`source-of-truth` is the most frequent code in the ledger (25 items) and — decisive for
theming — it recurs across BOTH parts and across topics she raised herself.

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** The flagship example is low-frequency by her own
account: umbrella policies change "maybe every four years... more likely you just get a
policy and stick with it" (E-068 region; skeptic lane §4d). The length of the answer is
itself a design problem she raised — pattern questions (annual gifting) need ~3 years of
history; a calendar cap would hide the pattern (E-068). And answer-freshness is a trust
cliff: stale output "would decrease trust a lot" (E-072).

**Interpretation** (researcher inference): the deep need is not "search" but **adjudication**
— the system taking responsibility for "current, with proof," which today is a human duty
distributed across six people. That's why dates and citations aren't garnish; they ARE the
feature. Frequency concern is likely misplaced at the class level (the *class* of
what's-current questions appears daily in her stories even if each instance type is rare),
but that is an inference requiring validation (07 §1.8).

**Product significance.** Highest of the session. It matches Ask's existing architecture but
demands the one primitive the code lacks: dates carried through retrieval + conflict
presentation (06 need #1/#2). **Confidence: high** (behavior-grounded, repeated, both parts,
minimal interviewer influence).

## T2 — Too many hands: information reaches its destination through fragile human relays, and everyone is afraid of being the one who drops it

**Central idea.** Every meeting outcome travels advisor → template notes → her → Wealthbox
tasks → Philip → RightCapital ("it's a lot of hands", E-049). Color travels Teams → maybe
Wealthbox → often nowhere (E-021, E-051). Documents travel Schwab → download → email → the
right (hopefully) accountant (E-094). Each relay leaks detail, adds delay, and — the
emotional core of the session — sustains "this horrible fear that we are going to be the one
that drops the ball" (E-053).

**Boundaries.** IN: handoff chains, SOS messages, Teams-vs-CRM fragmentation, 1099 delivery,
wrong-account errors, six-people-six-styles CRM entry (E-108). OUT: the approval design
answer (T3), the template itself (T4).

**Supporting evidence.** The full chain narrated step by step (E-046–E-049,
actual-behavior). SOS messages: "do you remember where so-and-so's kid is going to college?"
(E-024). Teams "holds more color" than the CRM and is a "disaster in terms of accurate
reporting and memory" (E-021, E-051-052 region). The 1099 "nail biter" with full PII
(E-094) and her ask for a programmatic client↔accountant link "so it couldn't mess up"
(E-095). The wrong-Roth-IRA change — a real past error rooted in ambiguous account
references (E-076). The proactive-call suppression effect: she avoids calling clients
because every call spawns homework routed through the same relays (E-064).

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** The relays exist partly ON PURPOSE: the advisor
sign-off (T3) and role boundaries ("I'm not going to touch your software" — respect, E-046)
are values, not accidents. She does not want the relay eliminated; she wants it **safer and
cheaper per hop**. Wrong-Roth and 1099 are single-story/seasonal (frequency unproven,
skeptic §4d).

**Interpretation.** The product's emotional job is dread reduction. Mechanically that means:
fewer manual re-entries (capture→approve→write), disambiguation where ambiguity caused harm
(accounts, recipients), and durable capture of what currently lives in Teams/memory.

**Product significance.** High — this theme justifies CRM write-back with assignee routing,
verified-recipient delivery, and quick-capture. **Confidence: high** for the pains
(behavior-grounded); **medium** for any specific fix (fix shapes were often co-authored with
the interviewer).

## T3 — The verification law: a human who was there approves, before anything moves; approval must be cheap because reviewer bandwidth is the scarcest resource

**Central idea.** Nothing derived from a meeting may reach the CRM, the client, or the
planning team until a human who was present green-lights it (E-027, E-036, E-038). Approval
is preferred over after-the-fact logs — explicitly, because she knows she'd never review a
log: "I've got a lot to do... that's maybe just one more thing to think about doing"
(E-062). And the review itself must be light: advisors are overwhelmed by long, wrong, or
mis-formatted AI output (E-038, E-039 — Chris abandons Jump's notes entirely and rewrites
from memory when the format breaks).

**Boundaries.** IN: approval-over-log, advisor-eyes gating, accuracy-as-precondition,
staleness-as-trust-collapse (E-072), AI file-write fears (E-058), review-bandwidth limits.
OUT: the internal/external content split (T4).

**Supporting evidence.** Ten `human-verification` items + seven `approval-over-log` items
spanning both parts. Her reasoning is causal, not vibes: Jump "comes in thinking that
someone's a stranger... like if you were to just overhear someone on the bus" (E-027) — the
AI lacks relationship context, so a context-holding human must arbitrate. Fear inventory:
deleting/overwriting files (E-058), wrong data "furthering" through systems (E-028), full
transcripts to clients as liability (E-034/E-035).

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** She simultaneously wants automation's benefits
(E-036, E-119-121) — the contradiction is resolved BY the approval gate, which is why it's a
law and not a preference. One true exception: low-stakes document self-filing got unreserved
enthusiasm with no verification caveat (E-009) — the law applies to *content that moves or
changes records*, not to organization. Note "this will never change something without asking"
phrasing was co-authored with the interviewer (skeptic §1b) — but the underlying preference
appears four separate times including unprompted contexts.

**Interpretation.** Design center: **interrupt-with-approval, sized to seconds** —
include/exclude checkboxes, small diffs, one Approve — and never a "review the log later"
posture. A second-person routing step (advisor approves → ops executes) mirrors the firm's
real structure.

**Product significance.** This is the constitution for every write feature; it also matches
the product's existing stance ("AI proposes, user approves") — one of the few places the
strategy and the participant already agree. **Confidence: high.**

## T4 — The template is sacred, the story is lost: meetings need two audiences and firm-shaped output

**Central idea.** Their 3-part note template (client action items / firm action items /
other notables) is deliberately minimal, client-trained, and non-negotiable — the firm
refused to flex it even when told flexibility would unlock automation, and Jump confirmed it
cannot convert their template into tasks (E-030, E-032, E-033). The cost is systematic loss
of the "human story": relational color, end-of-meeting reversals ("the last 10 minutes",
E-041), kid's-college details (E-024) — which they want captured in a SECOND, internal-only,
searchable artifact ("the inside scoop", E-050–E-052) that must never reach the client
(full-transcript liability, E-034).

**Boundaries.** IN: template fidelity, template→tasks, detail loss, internal/external
split, composable note review (E-043/E-044). OUT: which system stores the internal artifact
(she says Wealthbox — T5 coexistence).

**Supporting evidence.** "We're married to that note template... Jump... still can't take
your template and turn it into to-dos" (E-032, actual-behavior, includes the incumbent's
on-record failure). Client training: "clients have now been trained that you get a little
to-do list" (E-033). Detail loss "a lot of times stuff slips through the cracks" (E-025);
inside-scoop moments happen "all the time" (E-050 — one of the few explicit frequency
claims in the session). Her sketch of the fix is composable review: a base note plus
include/exclude add-ons, routable per item (E-043, E-044 — her own suggestion, hedged).

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** The two-outputs idea originated in the interviewer's
question ("have you ever tried to get Jump to give you two outputs?", skeptic §4a#1) — she
extended it with her own content, but the demand is co-authored. Her firm's template
specifics are probably idiosyncratic (skeptic §4f); what likely generalizes is *some* rigid
firm template + *some* internal/external split — needs replication. And the current inside
scoop handling (pinned notes, Teams, memory) is socially functional — the gap is
**recall**, not capture per se (skeptic §4e).

**Interpretation.** The wedge against Jump is not "better notes" — it's **fidelity to the
firm's own format plus safe routing** (template→tasks with assignees, internal lane) — the
exact standoff the incumbent has publicly failed to resolve for them.

**Product significance.** High; defines the meetings roadmap (06 needs #10-12).
**Confidence: high** on template lock-in and detail loss (behavior); **medium** on the
two-artifact solution shape (co-authored).

## T5 — Wealthbox is the firm's operating system: coexist and write back now; its weaknesses are real but displacement is not this session's mandate

**Central idea.** Everything routes through Wealthbox — notes, tasks-as-information
(E-074/E-075), workflows, pinned notes as permanent memory (E-073). It is "such an integral
part" (E-109), and simultaneously dumb in specific, structural ways: keyword-brittle search
(E-019), no email search (E-020), tasks with no real prioritization (E-119), template edits
that don't propagate to ~40 open workflows (E-098/E-099), advisors bypassing templates and
creating rework (E-092/E-093), six people entering data "in their own rhyme and reason"
(E-108).

**Boundaries.** IN: write-back requirements (into the firm's real containers: notes, tasks
with assignees, workflows), the help-agent need ("how do we do X here", E-091/E-092),
workflow-staleness. OUT: actually replacing the CRM (explicitly parked — see below).

**Supporting evidence.** As cited; all actual-behavior, high confidence.

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** Her "I've been kind of hoping that you would just
replace Wealthbox and combine a lot of softwares into one" (E-110) is real pull — but the
ledger and skeptic lane both flag it: it contradicts her own descriptions of Wealthbox as
integral (E-106–E-109), rests on a founder-spouse's hope plus a non-technical "it's pretty
basic" guess (E-111), and she is not the buyer. Her prediction that Jump becomes a full CRM
within five years (E-112) is competitive opinion, not evidence.

**Interpretation.** Near-term: Lantern must be the intelligence ON TOP of Wealthbox —
reading everything (including workflows and custom fields, which today's code doesn't
ingest), writing back into the firm's actual structures behind approval. Long-term: the task
/process layer's weaknesses are genuine whitespace, but entering it is a strategic decision
requiring more than one witness.

**Product significance.** Directs connector depth (workflow/custom-field ingestion,
assignee-aware task writes) and parks the replace-the-CRM ambition. **Confidence: high** for
coexist-and-write-back; **low** for any replacement appetite.

## T6 — Guarded proactivity: yes to triage, nudges and reports; no to anything that can be visibly stale

**Central idea.** She welcomes AI that proactively REASONS OVER live data — resorting her
task list around upcoming meetings (E-119/E-120), capacity honesty ("you can at max get six
of these done today, there's 21 assigned" — she accepted the framing even after calling it
potentially shaming, E-121), engagement-gap reports ("this client hasn't had any interaction
with [the firm] for such amount of time... would be really great", E-088), who's-the-hot-
client resource/fee-mismatch reports (E-124, E-129). But she distrusts maintained artifacts
whose currency can silently rot: the cash-needs map "would be hard to keep up to date"
(E-071/E-072) and a stale map destroys trust in the whole system.

**Boundaries.** IN: task triage, scheduling nudges (E-084: "here's all the people who
haven't scheduled... do you want me to just send them"), standing reports, manager
visibility (E-123). OUT: fact reconciliation on demand (T1).

**Supporting evidence & provenance caveats.** The pains are hers and behavior-grounded:
overdue reds, equal-visual-weight tasks, spelling-task-looks-like-fraud-task (E-119), the
judgment that lives only in her and Andy's heads (E-122), the scheduling cascade with
Calendly's rules forcing ~40% manual Outlook booking (E-080–E-086). The SOLUTIONS are
substantially co-authored: "AI coach" branding is hers (E-119) but arrived inside the
interviewer's pitch arc; manager reports and hot-client reports she floated with explicit
uncertainty ("I don't know what exactly we would do with that", E-124; skeptic §4b). The
ask-vs-structured preference (E-125) was textbook-led ("I think I know the answer already");
the durable part is the persona split — she wants ask-and-get, Seattle wants
browse-and-categorize (E-116, E-125/E-126).

**Interpretation.** The rule that reconciles this theme with T3: **proactivity is welcome
when it's derived live from sources at the moment of viewing (compute-on-view) and framed as
a proposal; it's dangerous when it's a stored artifact that claims to be current.**

**Product significance.** Medium-high. Justifies scheduled/standing report *generation*
architecture with visible dating; warns against maintained dashboards. **Confidence:
medium** overall (pain high, shapes co-authored, generalizability untested).

## T7 — The credibility economy: the firm sells being known, and the grunt work taxes exactly that

**Central idea.** Value, in her framing, is measured in client-perceived attentiveness:
never asking twice ("do I already know the answer to this? I'll go looking for it", E-014;
the doctor's-office cringe story, E-016; CC'd-email cringe, E-017), remembering the kid's
college for a hoodie-worthy gift (E-024), calling "just to check on you" (E-063). The
obstacles are battery-drains: follow-up chasing (E-011, E-115), document chasing that makes
her feel she's "bothering people" (E-089), scheduling relays (E-080-086), and the
no-reward-for-good-work dynamic where proactive care generates homework (E-064). Time saved
is explicitly earmarked for MORE human contact, not fewer staff (E-063) — "we need to
compete with [robo-advisors]... leverage AI to do the grunt work... more of the human
touches... more sticky business" (E-065).

**Boundaries.** IN: intake's ask-once registry (E-014-016 — the hardest-landing intake
feature), client-experience motives, positioning language. OUT: the portal/to-do-list
product shape (thin, reactive — E-113/E-114).

**Supporting evidence.** As cited — mostly actual-behavior or her own unprompted framing;
"never ask a client the same question twice" drew the strongest single positive of the
session ("oh I love that") with her own causal story attached.

**Contradicting/qualifying evidence.** Her "AI team member / historian / bridge" positioning
suggestions (E-057) are hers but arrive from a spouse who has absorbed the product vision
(skeptic §4h). The client-portal enthusiasm is comparative ("some softwares do X") and
shallow (E-113/E-114).

**Interpretation.** Positioning should sell the credibility outcome ("your firm always knows
its clients — with proof") rather than efficiency or privacy inputs. This is also the theme
that makes intake's answer-registry strategically important beyond onboarding.

**Product significance.** Medium-high, mostly for positioning, demo narrative, and intake.
**Confidence: high** for the motive; **medium** for specific positioning language.

## Demoted candidate themes (and why — preserved for honesty)

**D1 — "The Client Map is the answer to scattered truth."** The lead's initial memo carried
map-shaped opportunities (cash-needs map, account-purpose map). The skeptic lane's provenance
audit showed the "map" artifact framing was interviewer-introduced across every instance
("does this deserve its own map?"), she adopted the word only after it was supplied, hedged
repeatedly ("I don't know if that's worth building", E-071), and pre-named its fatal risk
(staleness, E-072). The underlying PAINS distribute into T1 (what's current) and T2 (where
things live); the map-as-artifact is a product hypothesis, not a finding. This demotion has
real consequences for the flagship narrative — see 00 and 05.

**D2 — "Whole-practice institutional memory" (why-did-we-decide-X, compliance tentacles,
process help-agent).** Real, hers, and vivid (E-091–E-093, E-096–E-099) — but discussed
briefly, without frequency evidence, and part solution-seeded (E-097). Folded as satellites
into T5 (help agent, workflow staleness) and T6 (change-impact as a proactive sweep) rather
than standing as a theme. Worth one follow-up question in the next interviews, not a bet.
