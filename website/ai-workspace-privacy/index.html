AI workspace privacy: a data flow guide for attorneys, CPAs, and consultants.

Updated 2026-04-27 · 12 min read

The short version. Most AI workspace tools fall into one of three privacy postures. Cloud workspaces (Notion AI, ChatGPT Plus, Reflect) store your data on their servers and route AI through their pipeline. BYOK desktop tools (Keepance, Cursor, Continue.dev) keep your data on your machine and route AI calls directly to your chosen provider with your key. Fully local AI (Ollama, LM Studio) keeps everything on your machine, including the model. The right posture depends on the threat you actually care about. This page maps each.

I'm going to walk through where your data actually goes when you use an AI workspace tool. Not the marketing version. The technical version, with the specific terms of service that govern each path.

Most attorneys, CPAs, and consultants I talk to are vaguely uncomfortable with putting client files and confidential work product into ChatGPT, but haven't read the terms closely enough to know what's actually true. So they either trust the marketing language ("we don't sell your data") or they don't use AI on the sensitive work at all, which means they leave a lot of value on the table. There's a better posture, and it starts with knowing the actual data flow. For attorneys, the stakes are especially concrete: ABA Formal Opinion 512 addresses competent use of AI, and United States v. Heppner (Judge Rakoff, S.D.N.Y., Feb. 17, 2026) held that consumer AI use without attorney direction offers no privilege protection.

What's in your AI workspace, really

Before talking about where the data goes, take stock of what's actually in there. For an attorney, CPA, or consultant, an AI workspace typically holds:

  • Client files and case notes. Matter summaries, case strategy, client communications, privileged research. For attorneys this is attorney-client privileged material. For tax professionals, this is IRC §7216-protected client data.
  • Confidential work product. Draft memos, contract markups, tax work papers, due diligence notes. Work protected by professional privilege or NDA obligations.
  • Client financial data. Tax returns, financial statements, audit findings, compensation details. Third-party information clients expect you to protect.
  • Engagement materials. Engagement letters, billing notes, matter management, deadline tracking. Contains client names, matter descriptions, and fee arrangements.
  • Internal professional materials. Practice development notes, peer reviews, sensitive operational content you wouldn't want appearing in a vendor's database.

Most of this is subject to professional confidentiality obligations, privilege protections, or explicit regulatory requirements. The privacy posture for AI workspaces is often far weaker than what these obligations demand. Closing that gap is what this page is about.

The three postures

Every AI workspace falls into one of three categories. Knowing which category you're using is the first move.

Posture 1: Cloud workspace with bundled AI

Examples: Notion AI, ChatGPT Plus, Claude.ai Pro, Reflect, Mem.ai, Tana.

Data flow:

  1. You type a message into the web or desktop app
  2. The app sends it to the workspace company's servers
  3. The workspace company stores it in their database
  4. The workspace company forwards it to an AI provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, or their own model)
  5. The response comes back through the workspace company
  6. The workspace company logs both, then shows you the response

Who has a copy: the workspace company, the AI provider, you. Three parties.

Who can see your prompt: workspace company employees with access (typically gated by SOC 2 controls), AI provider employees with access (similar), and anyone the workspace company sells to or shares with under their TOS.

What changes if you cancel: data deletion is governed by the workspace company's retention policy. Notion retains data for 30 days post-deletion; other tools vary.

Posture 2: Local-first desktop tool with BYOK

Examples: Keepance, Cursor (with BYOK), Continue.dev, Obsidian + AI plugins.

Data flow:

  1. You type a message into the desktop app
  2. The app reads your API key from your OS keychain
  3. The app sends the message directly to the AI provider's API endpoint, using your key
  4. The AI provider processes the request
  5. The response comes back to your machine
  6. The app writes the conversation to a file on your hard drive in a folder you chose

Who has a copy: you (the file on your machine) and the AI provider (in their API logs). Two parties.

Who can see your prompt: you and the AI provider. The desktop app's company is not in the path. There are no app-company servers seeing your data.

What changes if you cancel: nothing. Your files are on your machine, in Markdown. The desktop app uninstalling doesn't delete your data; you'd have to delete the folder yourself.

Posture 3: Fully local AI

Examples: Ollama running Llama 3 or Mistral; LM Studio with any open-weight model; Keepance with the Ollama provider configured.

Data flow:

  1. You type a message into the desktop app
  2. The app sends it to the local model running on your machine (typically on localhost:11434 for Ollama)
  3. The model generates a response, on your machine
  4. The response comes back to the app
  5. The app writes it to a file on your hard drive

Who has a copy: you. One party.

Who can see your prompt: you.

This is the strictest posture. The trade-off is model quality: open-weight models in 2026 are competitive with cloud frontier models on many tasks but lag on others (long-form strategic synthesis, very long context, latest knowledge cutoffs).

What each AI provider's policy actually says

Here is the specific TOS / policy language for each provider, with the version that applies to BYOK (API tier) usage. Read these for yourself; they update.

Anthropic (Claude)

  • Commercial API: Anthropic does not use API inputs or outputs to train models. See Commercial Terms of Service.
  • Logging: Anthropic retains API logs to monitor for abuse, with limited retention windows.
  • Consumer Claude.ai: Different terms. May use opted-in data per the consumer Privacy Policy.
  • Subpoena exposure: US-based provider, can be compelled to produce data via legal process.

OpenAI

  • API: OpenAI does not train on API inputs by default. See API data usage policies.
  • Logging: Default retention is 30 days for abuse monitoring; zero-retention available on request for some plans.
  • Consumer ChatGPT: Different terms. Free and Plus users opt out of training in Settings → Data Controls; default may be opt-in.
  • Subpoena exposure: US-based provider.

Google Gemini (via AI Studio)

  • Free tier: Per Gemini API terms, Google may use prompts to improve products on the unpaid tier.
  • Paid tier: Different terms; review the current version before relying on the free tier for anything sensitive.
  • Subpoena exposure: US-based provider.

The bigger picture

For high-sensitivity content (anything you'd be uncomfortable seeing in a court filing), the lowest-exposure posture is local Ollama. The next-lowest is BYOK with Anthropic API. After that, BYOK with OpenAI API. Notion AI and consumer ChatGPT Plus are higher-exposure than any of those.

For lower-sensitivity content (drafting blog posts, brainstorming features, working through a stuck pitch), any of these postures is reasonable. The cost of strict privacy is convenience and sometimes quality. Pick the right posture for the actual document.

Threat models: what are you protecting against?

Privacy talk gets vague when there's no specific threat in mind. Three concrete ones for attorneys, CPAs, and consultants, ranked by likelihood:

Threat 1: Vendor terms-of-service drift

The most likely threat is also the most boring. The vendor changes their data policy in a way you don't notice. They update the consumer-tier training-data clause. They get acquired. They sunset a feature. Your data was fine yesterday and is being used differently tomorrow.

Mitigation: keep the data in a posture where the vendor can't unilaterally change the rules on stuff that's already on your hard drive. Local-first BYOK accomplishes this.

Threat 2: Vendor breach

A workspace company has a security incident. Your data is among what's exfiltrated. This is rare but not unheard of (Notion, Asana, Confluence, Slack have all had incidents in the last decade).

Mitigation: the only data the workspace company has is the data you sent to them. With local-first BYOK, that's nothing. With cloud workspace, it's everything.

Threat 3: Subpoena / legal process

Less likely for most professionals but real. A regulator, opposing counsel, or law enforcement requests records. Whatever the workspace company holds is potentially producible. Whatever you hold on your machine is also potentially producible, but the path is different and the protections (attorney-client privilege, fifth amendment for self-incrimination) are stronger.

Mitigation: keep workspace data local; AI provider sees only the specific text you sent for inference, not the whole archive.

The threat we are not protecting against

Determined targeted state-actor attacks. Defending against those is nation-state-grade work, beyond any consumer software. If you genuinely have that threat model (security researcher, dissident, journalist on certain beats), you need air-gapped machines and operational security beyond an "AI workspace privacy guide."

Practical privacy checklist

For an attorney, CPA, or consultant using AI in 2026, here's the working checklist:

  1. Pick your AI provider's API tier (BYOK), not their consumer chatbot. Better default privacy posture, no training on your data by default.
  2. Use a local-first desktop tool that keeps your conversation history on your machine. Keepance, Cursor with BYOK, Obsidian with an AI plugin. Avoid putting client files in a cloud workspace's database.
  3. Set a hard monthly cap on each AI provider's billing. Limits damage if a key leaks.
  4. Encrypt the disk. Both macOS (FileVault) and Windows (BitLocker) make this trivial. If your laptop is stolen, the workspace folder is unreadable.
  5. Don't store API keys in plaintext. Use the OS keychain (macOS) or an encrypted file (Windows / Linux) inside the desktop app.
  6. For very sensitive content, use local Ollama. Anything you genuinely can't risk touching any server: draft client filings, privileged strategy notes, tax return data with client names.
  7. Don't send client PII without redacting first. If the client didn't consent to having their name processed by an AI provider, redact before sending. This matters especially for attorneys under Model Rule 1.6 and CPAs under IRC §7216.
  8. Read each provider's TOS once a year. They change. Calendar reminder, 15 minutes, done.

How Keepance implements the local-first BYOK posture

Specifically, here's what Keepance does:

  • No Keepance account. No signup. No password.
  • No Keepance servers in the data path. The desktop app talks directly to Anthropic / OpenAI / Google / your local Ollama. Keepance's server-side infrastructure (the marketing site, the license validator, the form handler) never sees your prompts or your files.

Frequently asked questions

Does ChatGPT train on my conversations?

Consumer ChatGPT (chatgpt.com) trains on conversations by default for free and Plus users; you can opt out in Settings → Data Controls. The OpenAI API (which powers BYOK tools like Keepance) does not train on inputs by default. Source: OpenAI's API data usage policy.

Does Claude train on my conversations?

Anthropic's commercial API (BYOK) does not train on inputs by default per their commercial terms. Claude.ai consumer tier may use opted-in conversations. Anthropic's policy is generally stricter than OpenAI's at the consumer tier.

Does Notion AI see my workspace data?

Yes. Notion stores all workspace data on Notion's servers, and Notion AI processes that data through its model providers (OpenAI and Anthropic) to generate responses. Per Notion's documentation, customer data is not used to train these third-party models. But the data lives on Notion's servers regardless of AI use.

Is a local-first AI tool actually private?

Local-first AI tools store your conversations on your machine and don't have their own servers in the data path. The only network call goes from your machine directly to your chosen AI provider, using your own API key. The tool's company never sees your prompts. This is more private than any cloud workspace, but the AI provider still sees the specific text you choose to send for inference.

What about local models like Ollama? Are they fully private?

Yes. With Ollama or LM Studio running open-weight models, the AI itself runs on your machine. Nothing leaves your device. The trade-off is quality: open-weight models lag behind Claude / GPT / Gemini on long-form analytical and legal work. Most attorneys and CPAs use a hybrid: BYOK Claude for high-stakes tasks, local Ollama for volume tasks where 90% quality is fine.

Should I worry about the AI provider being subpoenaed?

All major US AI providers can be compelled to produce data via subpoena or court order. The retention windows differ: OpenAI keeps API logs for 30 days by default; Anthropic for similar windows. Local-first tools don't have this exposure for the data on your machine, only for the specific text you sent to the AI provider during inference.

What if my laptop gets stolen?

Encrypt the disk (FileVault on macOS, BitLocker on Windows). With disk encryption, a stolen machine reveals nothing. The API keys in the OS keychain are protected by your account password, separately from the disk encryption.
