## The story so far (January to June 2026)

### It started as a notes app for solo founders (late January 2026)

Advisor Prep Hero was not born as Advisor Prep Hero. The first version, built starting **28 January 2026**, was called **Projelli**. It was an AI workspace aimed at indie founders and solo builders: a place to keep your notes where every conversation you had with the AI turned into a real file you owned and could edit later. Two ideas were baked in from day one and never left: your files live on **your** machine (not on someone else's cloud), and you bring your **own** AI key, so the company never holds your data or your keys. Within a couple of weeks it grew from a browser tool into a proper desktop app you install on Windows or Mac.

### It learned to handle real documents and remember your stuff (April 2026)

Through the spring it got a lot more capable. By **mid-April 2026** it could open and edit real Office files (Word, Excel, PowerPoint), it auto-updated itself, and it picked up four headline powers in the **v1.5** release: it could **remember and search your own files** so the AI could answer from them, it could plug into **other AI tools** you already used, it let the **AI edit a document side-by-side** with you so you accepted only the changes you liked, and it could run **fully offline on a free local AI model** plus take voice input. A follow-up **v2.0** push in **early May 2026** rounded out the gaps (reading PDFs and images, a plugin system, the AI reading answers aloud, and support for Spanish and German). At this point it was a genuinely useful private AI workspace, but it was still pitched at founders and still had almost no users.

### The first big repositioning: from founders to confidential professionals (late May 2026)

In **late May 2026** came the first major turn. The team realized "indie founders" was a crowded, hard-to-reach audience, and that the product's real superpower (keeping sensitive work private on your own machine) mattered far more to people who handle **confidential client work**. So the whole thing was repositioned for professionals whose job is secrecy: **lawyers first**, then tax preparers and CPAs, then consultants. On **29 May 2026** the app was renamed from Projelli to **Advisor Prep Hero** (v2.1), got a new look (a navy shield instead of the old jelly-bean), new pricing tiers, and "profession packs" of ready-made templates for each kind of work.

### Sharpening trust and adding email (early June 2026)

Early June was about making the product honest and trustworthy enough for a professional to rely on. A run of releases added **verification banners** (when the AI produces a citation, it gets flagged "check this before you rely on it," because AI can make things up), a real **export pipeline** to Word, PDF, and PowerPoint, and an integrity pass that scrubbed any marketing claim the product couldn't actually back up. Then, on **8 June 2026**, **email came to Advisor Prep Hero**: you could pull your Outlook, Gmail, or other email into Advisor Prep Hero, where it was locked up safely on your own machine and made searchable, never routed through a company server.

### Advisor Prep Hero 3.0: it grew up into a real platform (9 June 2026)

**9 June 2026** was the biggest single moment so far. Advisor Prep Hero **3.0** turned a solo-user experiment into a real, professional product, and re-described it as **"the private intelligence layer for a law practice."** The headline pieces:
- Its **own Word engine**, built from scratch, with tracked changes and an **"AI redline"** that revises a document from a plain-English instruction, leaving every edit as a tracked change the lawyer accepts or rejects.
- **Answers grounded in your own files, with clickable citations**, kept strictly separate per client so one client's documents can never leak into another's.
- An **honest trust layer**: a live indicator that always tells you exactly where your data is about to go, a printable "Data Map" you can show a worried client, and a choice between keeping everything **fully on your machine**, sending it straight to your own AI provider, or routing it through a no-storage proxy.
- A **firm tier** where several lawyers can collaborate, with the shared data scrambled end-to-end so the relay in the middle can never read it.
- It shipped as **proper signed installers** for Windows, Mac, and Linux, with automatic updates and real paid subscriptions.

The next few weeks (3.1 and 3.2, through **mid-June**) polished and completed this: the firm tier became fully usable, a nasty memory bug that could crash the app was fixed, and big new capabilities landed (single sign-on for firms, an **encrypted vault** for your files, the ability to **read scanned PDFs**, and **live multi-person editing** of the same Word document).

### The honest reckoning: a good product almost nobody was using (17 June 2026)

By **17 June 2026** the team stopped and looked hard at the numbers, and they were sobering. Despite all this genuinely strong software, there were essentially **zero paying customers, almost no website visitors, and no sales.** The uncomfortable conclusion: the thing holding Advisor Prep Hero back was **not** the engineering. It was **trust and distribution.** A law firm won't buy serious software from someone they've never heard of with no references and no track record, no matter how good it is. The call was to stop piling on features, fix every claim that overpromised, and start selling to real people by hand. *(A note for honesty: a later round of hands-on testing showed the product still had rough, unfinished corners too, so "just go sell it" was a bit too tidy. Finishing the product and finding customers turned out to be work that had to happen together.)*

### The big pivot: from lawyers to financial advisors (23 June 2026)

The most important turn came on **23 June 2026**. While doing the sales rethink, the team noticed something they'd been walking past: the one person who had described **real, unprompted, "I would use this every single day" pain** was not a litigator at all. It was a **financial advisor** (Jameson's wife). She needed to know everything about a household before each meeting, and was stitching that picture together by hand across eight different tools every week.

That changed everything. Financial advisors turned out to be a much better fit than lawyers: they have real software budgets, a **cleaner privacy story** (the rules that govern them are about safeguarding client data, which Advisor Prep Hero already nails, rather than the thornier world of legal privilege), and, crucially, **a warm first customer already existed** instead of a cold list of strangers. So Advisor Prep Hero re-aimed at advisors. The organizing idea shifted from a legal **"matter"** (one case) to a **"client" or "household"** (the family an advisor looks after) — a shift from one-off legal jobs to ongoing relationships. Importantly, this was mostly a change of **words and framing**: under the hood the engine stayed exactly the same, only the labels and the story changed.

Around the pivot, Advisor Prep Hero also clarified that it is **not** trying to replace the tools advisors already use (their CRM, their planning software). It's the private layer **underneath** all of them that finally holds the *whole* picture of a client and answers questions about it with citations. A read-only connector to **Wealthbox** (a popular advisor CRM) was designed so Advisor Prep Hero could pull a client's real records in.

### Where Advisor Prep Hero stands today (28 June 2026)

The strategic call for the next stretch is simple and deliberately narrow: **prove that real advisors will actually use this every week, and that at least one or two will pay.** No new features for now. The plan is to get Jameson's wife's firm using it weekly first, then a handful of other advisors.

To support that, the last days of June brought a big **redesign of how you move around the app**: it was simplified to **three clear tabs (Client Map, Ask, and Workflows)**, the per-client view was fixed so you stay inside one client's world, and the "Ask" screen was reshaped to feel like a familiar chat app. The app was also scrubbed to lead with advisors (a sample advisor household is what you now see first), and a realistic set of fake advisor data was seeded so demos feel real.

The bottom line as of **28 June 2026**: the code is healthy and green, there's a **live web demo** anyone can click through, and the real app **passed a careful three-times-in-a-row test on real Windows hardware with zero bugs** — the strongest health signal in months. Nothing has shipped to outside users yet; it's still pre-launch. The whole game now is turning the first real advisors into weekly, paying users.

### 29 June 2026 — Board decision: go for the lead, as a simple AI app (not a note-taker)

A deep look at the main competitor, **Jump** (a well-funded incumbent, the most-used advisor-AI tool), came back with a cautious recommendation: don't fight Jump head-on for advisors; retreat to a narrower corner Jump doesn't own. Jameson — wearing the board hat — **disagreed, and overruled it.** He signed up for Jump himself, went through the actual product and their AI videos, and came away unimpressed: Jump's AI felt messy and scattered, hard to find and hard to follow, and the product is still a **note-taking app at its core** with a fuzzy AI direction ("connect 60 things, then send forms and take notes" — not "connect your files and ask questions"). His call: **keep going for the top spot in financial-advisor AI**, and win by being the opposite of Jump — **simpler and more powerful**, a clean *connect-your-files → ask-cited-questions → living-Client-Map* **AI app, not a note-taker.** No paying clients yet, and the "go get real users, don't just build" discipline still holds — but the *direction* is now set: lead the category as an AI-first product. (Full decision: `docs/strategy/2026-06-29-board-decision-leading-advisor-ai.md`.)

### 29 June 2026 — A real-world test with real cloud files caught a sneaky bug (and we fixed it right)

The earlier "passed three times on Windows" test used the app's own sample data. This time we made it harder and more honest: a realistic fake firm (Northcrest, 26 households) with **real cloud files connected through OneDrive**, then drove the app like an advisor would on a real Windows laptop. That's the kind of test that finds the problems a polished demo hides — and it found one that mattered. When you opened a single client, their **Documents list came up empty even though the files were sitting right there on the computer.** The app had actually loaded everything; the bug was in the *filter* that decides which files belong to which client — it was comparing two slightly different ways of writing a folder's location and so matched nothing. We fixed it the robust way, not with a patch: the filter now uses the exact same "which client owns this file" rules the rest of the app already trusts, so every client sees their own files and never another client's — even on computers that treat "Acme" and "acme" as two different folders. Two smaller fixes rode along: clicking "Client Map" now takes you back to the client list, and a client's "recent questions" no longer shows the same question twice. The lesson, again: test on the real thing, with real data.

### 29 June 2026 — Redesigning the Client Map, the product's front door

The **Client Map** — the one-page picture of a household, and the first thing an advisor sees — is being redesigned. Two ideas drive it. First, **make it feel like the Ask screen**: clean, familiar, and chat-like, instead of a dense form. Second, **base what it shows on real research** into what an advisor actually needs to know before a client meeting, rather than categories we guessed at. That's the board's "simple and powerful, AI-first, not a cluttered note-taker" direction made concrete on the most important screen. This is a design direction now in progress, not something that has shipped yet — but it's where the front door of the product is heading.

### 29 June 2026 — Ask grew up: from a strict search box into a smart assistant that still proves itself

Until now, **Ask** was deliberately narrow: it only answered from the files you'd loaded, and if it found nothing it just said "I couldn't find anything." Safe, but it often felt dumb — a new advisor would type a normal question and hit a wall. We changed Ask into a genuinely smart assistant you can chat with like ChatGPT: it answers general financial-planning questions, helps you draft emails and one-pagers, and goes back and forth — saying yes to almost everything. The **one thing it still won't do is search the live internet** (that stays out, for privacy). The hard part was protecting the thing that makes Advisor Prep Hero special: when it answers from *your* client files, every claim is **cited and checkable**. The fix is that every answer now wears a **label showing what it's built from** — green "From your files" (cited), grey "General guidance" (the AI's own knowledge, clearly marked, with a quiet reminder that rules and figures change so verify them), or blue "Draft." A cited fact and a general opinion are never blended into the same paragraph, so you can always tell which is which, and the Sources list only ever fills with your own files. The honest promise replaces the old "every answer is cited" with the truer, stronger "every answer **from your files** is cited, and Ask always shows you what it's using." A nervous compliance team can flip a **Files-only switch** to turn the general help off entirely and run Ask exactly as before. This was built to a written proposal Jameson approved, and pressure-tested with a second, independent AI that arrived at the same design. (Full design: `ASK-SMART-AGENT-PROPOSAL.md`.)

### 2026-07-02 — Green light to plan the "Lantern-Plus" program: matching Jump, the private way

This work happens in a separate parallel copy of the product ("lantern-plus") so the standalone version can be demoed and released undisturbed. Detailed build plans were written for the implementation agents; nothing new has shipped yet.

### 2026-07-02 — Design phase for Lantern-Plus: the story leads, and meetings live on the client

Two decisions came out of his review. First, meetings get their own tab inside each client's page (next to Documents, Email, and Activity), keeping the product's one rule intact: everything lives on the client, and the app never becomes a note-taker at its core. Second, all the new features will be finished before any advisor sees them, rather than shipping the smaller pieces first. The whole experience is now being laid out as one story: a day in an advisor's life, from the morning meeting list to the evening review.

The meeting-recording feature is genuinely done. Two new trust-first capabilities also entered the picture at Jameson's direction, sparked by a certified financial planner's sharp question — "how do meeting participants KNOW they're being recorded without a bot?": the **Recording Notice Kit** shipped (the app now verifies from its own transcript that the advisor actually said the recording notice out loud, and files the proof), and the **Notice Card** — a local, records-nothing participant that joins the meeting to show everyone a recording notice — is in build. The bot-less recorder's biggest objection is being turned into its most visible trust feature.

## 2026-07-04 (late) — The Notice Card is built: turning "no bot in the meeting" into our biggest trust feature

The Notice Card, in build earlier today, is now built (v1 + v2). Competitors put a recording bot into the call so everyone can see they're being recorded — and that bot exists to carry the meeting's words off to their cloud. Our whole promise is the opposite: nothing joins the call, nothing leaves the advisor's machine. That was a privacy win but it left a gap a real financial planner pointed out — without something visible in the meeting, participants rely on the advisor remembering to say "I'm recording." So we built the Notice Card: a second participant that runs entirely on the advisor's own computer, joins the meeting as "⏺ Recording Notice — Sarah," and shows everyone a calm little card saying the meeting is being recorded and that the recording stays on Sarah's computer — nothing is uploaded. It records nothing, sends nothing, and it leaves the moment recording stops, which is itself an honest "recording has ended" signal no competitor can offer.

The clever part that made it cheap: because we own the little window the card runs in, we hand the meeting a "camera" that's actually just a web page we draw — the notice card with a live "Recording 12:34" timer — so there's no fussy camera-driver software to build. It joins Teams and Zoom the same way a guest with a browser does (Google Meet needs a sign-in, so there we honestly fall back to "say the notice aloud"). And nothing about it can ever break a recording: the card is always optional, always offered from the calendar so there's no setup, and if it can't get in, the app just reminds the advisor to say the notice out loud. The advisor's spoken notice — which the app already verifies from its own transcript — remains the compliance backbone; the card is the visible, in-everyone's-face proof on top. It went through nine rounds of independent adversarial review that caught (and we fixed) real issues — a privacy leak where an audio-only request could have reached the real microphone, an offer that could target the wrong client's concurrent meeting, and several honesty fixes so the app never overstates that the card "covered the whole meeting." It turns the industry's favorite objection to bot-less recording ("but then nobody knows") into the most visible trust feature in the category.

## 2026-07-05 — Direction call: stop hunting for every bug, lock down the "trust-breakers," and get it in front of one real advisor

After a long run of finding-and-fixing bugs, Jameson asked the honest question: every time we review, we find more — where does this end? The answer, backed by the actual bug list: a complex app never reaches zero bugs, so "bug-free" is the wrong finish line. The scary early bugs (losing a document, one client's data leaking into another's, crashing on Windows) were mostly already killed, the newest finds were getting smaller, and the single most-feared bug — could one client's private documents leak into another client's answers? — was tested hard and turned out **not to exist**. So Jameson made the call: **stop broad bug-hunting; instead lock down the short list of "trust-breakers" — the handful of things that, if they happened to the very first real advisor, would end the relationship (lose their document, leak a client, crash on their computer, a connector that lies about importing, files that don't show up) — prove the app is stable, and then put it in front of one real advisor.** The reasoning: a week of real use will teach us more than the fortieth lab hunt. This flips the whole effort from "find any bug anywhere" to "make the trust-breakers airtight, then ship to a human." The finish-line checklist lives in `coordination/TRUST-BREAKER-LOCKDOWN.md`.

## 2026-07-05 (later) — A concrete DEMO V1: the exact first thing to make work end-to-end

Jameson turned the fuzzy "get it testable" goal into a precise picture — a one-page requirements graphic for **Demo V1**. It's a tight, ordered path of six things that must work together, and naming them made the whole effort sharper: **connect an AI (ChatGPT and the built-in on-device AI), connect your data (Outlook, OneDrive, Wealthbox), see real import progress, ask questions across that data — even while it's still loading, record a Teams meeting with a visible in-meeting "recording" card, and search the meeting transcript with AI.** A few things he'd mentioned before (like AI-drafted emails) were deliberately left *out* of this first demo, which made it smaller and faster.
