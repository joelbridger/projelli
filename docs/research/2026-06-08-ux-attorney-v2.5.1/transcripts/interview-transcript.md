## Section 4: AI: use, attitudes, and the confidentiality line

**[0:34]**

**LW:** I want to shift to AI now. Have you used any AI tools for work? Tell me about that.

**DM:** [exhales] Yes, and it's a fraught little story. So in 2024, everybody's talking about ChatGPT, my nephew won't shut up about it, and I'm curious, because I'm not a Luddite, I want to be clear about that, I'm skeptical but I'm not a dinosaur. So one night I tried the free version. And I had a demand letter to write, a fairly standard one, and I thought, let's see. So I gave it the gist, the facts, the ask, and it wrote me a demand letter. And Lena, I have to be honest with you, it was good. It was genuinely good. It was a better first draft than some first-years I've worked with. The structure was right, the tone was right, it was, I'd say, eighty percent of the way there. I sat back and I thought, oh. Oh no. This changes things.

**LW:** Tell me about that "oh no."

**DM:** The "oh no" was two things at once. One was, this is going to change my profession and I'd better understand it. But the second "oh no," the one that actually mattered, hit me about an hour later, lying in bed. I'd typed my client's facts into that box. My client's actual situation, names, the dispute, the numbers. And I lay there thinking, where did that go? I just took confidential client information, the thing my entire professional existence is built on protecting, and I typed it into a website owned by a company in California, and I have no idea what they did with it. And I felt sick. I genuinely felt sick. I tried ChatGPT, it wrote a beautiful demand letter, and then I lay awake wondering if I'd just handed my client's facts to a server in California. And the next morning I stopped. Cold. I haven't used it for anything real since.

**LW:** What specifically made you stop? Was it just that feeling, or was there something else?

**[0:38]**

**DM:** It was the feeling plus the knowledge that there were real consequences, not hypothetical ones. I'd read about the Avianca case, you probably know it, the lawyers who used ChatGPT and it invented fake cases, fake citations, hallucinated them out of thin air, and they filed them, and they got sanctioned and publicly humiliated. And I thought, that's two separate nightmares in one. There's the fabrication nightmare, the made-up cases. And there's the confidentiality nightmare, the where-did-my-data-go. Avianca was mostly the first kind. But it put the fear in me about all of it.

**LW:** You said "the more recent one" earlier, in passing. What did you mean?

**DM:** Right, there was a newer one, this year. The Heppner matter, United States v. Heppner, down in the Southern District of New York, this was February, a few months ago. A defense lawyer used ChatGPT and didn't disclose it, and the court came down hard, called the conduct reckless. Reckless. That word. For a lawyer, "reckless" is a career word, that's the kind of word that follows you. And so between Avianca and Heppner, the message I took, the message a lot of us took, is: this technology is powerful and it is a loaded gun, and if you handle it carelessly you will be the cautionary tale at the next bar CLE. And I am not going to be the cautionary tale at the next CLE. I've gone twenty-four years without a complaint. I'm not blowing that on a chatbot.

**LW:** When you think about using AI on actual client matters, what goes through your mind? Walk me through the thought process.

**DM:** Two voices, honestly. There's the envy voice and the fear voice and they argue constantly. The envy voice says, Diane, you saw what it did with that demand letter, imagine that pointed at your eighteen hundred pages, imagine your weekends back, the leverage here is enormous and your younger competitors are going to use it and eat your lunch if you don't. And then the fear voice says, yes, and one mistake, one confidentiality breach, one hallucinated citation you didn't catch, and it's your license, it's a malpractice claim, it's your name in the disciplinary reports that every other lawyer in Cleveland reads with their coffee. And the fear voice usually wins, because the downside is asymmetric. The upside is I save some time. The downside is I lose everything I've built. Those aren't the same size.

**LW:** I've heard lawyers talk about confidentiality with these tools. What does that mean to you, in practical terms?

**DM:** In practical terms it means Rule 1.6. It means I have a duty, an actual ethical duty with teeth, to protect information relating to the representation of a client. It's close to sacred, honestly, it's the foundation, it's the reason clients tell their lawyers the truth, because they know it stays with the lawyer. So when I think about confidentiality and these tools, I'm thinking, if I put client information into this thing, have I disclosed it? To whom? Could it come out? Could it be used to train something? Could it be subpoenaed from them? Could it leak? Every one of those is a way I've broken the thing I'm sworn to protect. So "confidentiality" with AI, to me, is the single gate. Everything else is features. That's the gate.

**LW:** When you use a tool "in the cloud," what's your understanding of where your information goes and who can see it?

**[0:42]**

**DM:** [pause] Honestly? And this is me being candid because you said there are no wrong answers, so don't laugh at me. My understanding is fuzzy. My working model, the gut-level one, is: if it goes to the cloud, it's not really mine anymore. It's on somebody else's computer somewhere, and somebody else's computer means somebody else can, in principle, get at it. That's the instinct. The cloud is just, you know, other people's computers, and my client's secrets are sitting on them.

**LW:** Tell me more about that. When you picture it, what's the picture?

**DM:** The picture is, I type something into a website or an app, and it travels off to a big building full of servers owned by some tech company, and it lives there, and they have it. And whether it's an email program or an AI chatbot, in my head it's kind of the same thing. The information left my office and went to live on their machines. So the AI thing and, like, storing my documents on someone's cloud, they sort of blur together for me. It's all "my stuff is on their servers now." I don't know that I draw a clean line between "this app is keeping my files on its computers" and "this thing is sending my question off to some AI company to get an answer." It's all just, it went out into the cloud, and the cloud is not my office, and not-my-office makes me nervous.

**LW:** So if I'm hearing you right, "the app stores my files on its servers" and "my question gets sent to an AI company" feel like roughly the same kind of thing to you. Is that fair?

**DM:** Yeah. Yeah, I think that's fair. I mean, are they different? They might be different, I'd believe you if you told me they were different. But in my head they live in the same scary box labeled "your client's stuff is somewhere you can't see it." I haven't sat down and parsed the technical difference, because frankly nobody's ever explained it to me in a way that stuck, and I'm busy, so the whole category just gets a big red "be careful" sticker on it and I move on.

**LW:** That's really helpful, thank you for being honest about it. [pause] What have you heard, from the bar, from colleagues, from the news, about AI and your professional duties?

**DM:** A fair amount, in a scattered way. The headline cases we talked about, Avianca and Heppner, those circulate, those are the campfire stories now.
