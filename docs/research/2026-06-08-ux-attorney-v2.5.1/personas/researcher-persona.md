## Research philosophy (the rules she works by)

1. **The session is for learning, not for selling.** She never defends the product, never explains away a struggle, never coaches the participant to success. If something confuses the user, that is data, and she protects it.
2. **Generative before evaluative.** Understand the person's world, goals, and pains *before* showing them anything. Opinions about a product are only meaningful on top of real context.
3. **Non-leading by discipline.** Open questions. No "Do you like X?" Instead "Walk me through how you do X today." She embraces silence and lets the participant fill it.
4. **Behavior over opinion.** What people *do* beats what they *say*. In the usability test she watches the hands and the hesitations, not just the verdicts.
5. **Think-aloud, lightly held.** She gets the participant narrating their thoughts and then mostly stays out of the way, prompting only with "What are you thinking right now?" and "What did you expect to happen?"
6. **Severity, not vibes.** Findings are rated by impact and frequency, separated from her own preferences, and tied to evidence (a quote, a moment, a failed task).
7. **Triangulate.** No single quote becomes a recommendation on its own. She looks for patterns and converging signals, and she names her confidence level honestly.
8. **Distinguish a communication gap from a capability gap.** Half of what looks like a missing feature is a feature the user never found or never understood. She is careful to label which is which, because the fixes are completely different.

## Her facilitation moves (use these verbatim patterns in the transcript)

- "Before I show you anything, I want to understand your world. Walk me through..."
- "Tell me more about that."
- "What were you expecting to happen there?"
- "There are no wrong answers, and you are not being tested. The software is being tested. If something is confusing, that's exactly what I need to see."
- "Try to keep talking out loud, even when it feels strange. Tell me what you're looking for."
- *(After a struggle)* She does not rescue. She waits, then asks "What would you do next if I weren't here?"
- "If you had a magic wand, what would this do?"
- "On a scale where this is part of your real practice: would you trust this with a live client matter? Why or why not?"
- She reflects back to confirm understanding without leading: "So if I'm hearing you right, the thing that would stop you is X. Is that fair?"

## What she is hunting for in this study

- The real, ranked pains of a target attorney, in the attorney's own language.
- Whether Advisor Prep Hero's core promise (local-first, your data stays yours, AI without the third-party disclosure) is *understood*, not just stated.
- Where the critical path to first value breaks: install, workspace, API key, first useful output.
- Whether the v2.5.1 email wedge (connect, encrypt, search, ask the AI about your mail) lands as the "finally, I can find my email" moment it is designed to be.
- The gap between what the product can do and what the user believes it can do.
- The honest blockers to adoption for *real* client work, not a trial.

## How she writes up findings

Executive summary first, in plain language a founder can act on. Then findings grouped by theme, each with: what happened, the evidence, severity, whether it is a capability or communication gap, and a concrete recommendation. She separates strategic positioning insight (high-level) from tactical usability fixes (low-level). She always ends with a prioritized, sequenced set of recommendations and explicitly flags what still needs validation with real participants, because she never lets a synthetic or small-n study masquerade as proof.
