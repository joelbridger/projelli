## ⚠️ Lead finding: *United States v. Heppner* is REAL (and it is about Claude)

This is the single most important correction to your current assumptions, so it goes first.

Your appendix marks *U.S. v. Heppner* as **UNVERIFIED** and notes you could not find it on CourtListener or Westlaw. **The case exists.** It postdates the training cutoff of most language models (it was decided in February 2026), which is almost certainly why you got back "no record" answers when you asked an AI to verify it, and why the citation felt fabricated. It is not fabricated. Verified details:

- **Full citation:** *United States v. Heppner*, No. 1:25-cr-00503-JSR (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026), Dkt. No. 27 (Rakoff, J.). Bench ruling Feb. 10, 2026; written memorandum opinion filed Feb. 17, 2026.
- **Parties/facts:** Bradley Heppner, an executive charged with securities and wire fraud (matters relating to Beneficient and GWG Holdings, Inc.), used the **consumer (non-enterprise) version of Anthropic's Claude** to generate ~31 documents analyzing his own defense strategy, then later shared them with his lawyers. Counsel had **not** directed him to do this.
- **Holding:** The AI-generated documents were **not** protected by attorney-client privilege **or** the work-product doctrine.

Judge Rakoff's reasoning rests on three orthodox privilege pillars, not on any new "AI rule":
1. **Not an attorney-client communication.** Claude is not a lawyer and was not engaged by counsel as an agent; a client's independent consultation of a third-party information source does not become privileged merely because the notes are later shown to a lawyer.
2. **Confidentiality defeated / waiver.** The court focused on **Claude's consumer terms of service and privacy policy**, under which Anthropic reserved rights to log prompts/outputs, use them for training, and disclose to third parties. Submitting information to a system whose terms expressly undermine confidentiality is inconsistent with a reasonable expectation of confidentiality, so any privilege was waived.
3. **No retroactive cloaking.** Forwarding a pre-existing, non-privileged document to counsel after the fact does not transform it into a privileged communication.
   Work product failed for a related reason: the materials were created on the defendant's **own initiative, not at counsel's direction**, and did not reveal counsel's strategy.

**Why this is, on balance, *good* news for Advisor Prep Hero — if you frame it precisely.** The court expressly signaled the opposite result is available on different facts. In dicta: *"Had counsel directed Heppner to use Claude, Claude might arguably be said to have functioned in a manner akin to a highly trained professional who may act as a lawyer's agent within the protection of the attorney-client privilege."* The decisive facts against Heppner were (a) a **consumer** tool with training-permissive terms, (b) a **non-lawyer** acting **without counsel's direction**. Advisor Prep Hero's intended posture is the mirror image: a **licensed professional** using a **provider API tier** (where training is contractually disabled and data is not used to improve models), under the professional's own direction and supervision. That is precisely the *Kovel* "agent of counsel" theory the court left open. See *United States v. Kovel*, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961).

**Do not overclaim it, though.** *Heppner* does **not** hold that "all AI use waives privilege," and it does **not** bless enterprise/API use. It is a single SDNY trial-court opinion, fact-bound, and already drawing academic criticism (a Harvard Law Review blog argues the opinion drifts toward over-broadly excluding client AI use and that a more fact-dependent analysis should sometimes find privilege). Treat it as **persuasive, on-point, and directionally favorable to your architecture story** — not as settled appellate law.

A precise, defensible way to characterize it in your own materials:

> *In* United States v. Heppner *(S.D.N.Y. 2026), the first ruling to address the question, a court held that documents a defendant created using a* consumer *AI chatbot — under terms that permitted retention and training, and without his lawyers' direction — were not privileged. The court's reasoning turned on the tool's confidentiality terms and the absence of attorney direction, and it expressly left open that counsel-directed use of a confidentiality-preserving tool could fall within privilege as an agent of counsel.*

Full treatment in **Q8–Q12** below.

# Part I — Legal ethics and professional responsibility

## I-A. Competence and technology (Model Rule 1.1)

**Q1.** **[HIGH]** Technological competence under Rule 1.1, Comment 8 (2012) as applied to GAI by ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024) is **a combination of all three** of your options, not any one. Opinion 512 requires that a lawyer acquire a **reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations** of the specific GAI tool used (your (a)), exercise **independent professional judgment and critically evaluate output** rather than abdicating judgment to the tool (your (b)), and — as a confidentiality predicate — understand **how the tool processes, retains, and may further use or disclose input data** (your (c)). The opinion is explicit that the depth of required verification is **task-specific**: a tool used to generate ideas requires less independent checking than a tool used for document review or factual assertions. It also states GAI competence is not yet *mandatory* — using GAI is not currently required for competent representation. The standard is "reasonable understanding," not engineering-level mastery; consulting someone who has the expertise satisfies it.

**Q2.** **[MEDIUM]** Disclosure to the client is **conditionally required, not categorically required**, and the trigger is Rule 1.4 (communication) and Rule 1.6 (confidentiality/informed consent), not Rule 1.1. Opinion 512's framework: disclosure/informed consent is required (i) before inputting a client's confidential information into a **self-learning** tool whose design risks that information surfacing elsewhere; (ii) when GAI output **will influence a significant decision** in the representation; (iii) when the **client asks**, or the **engagement agreement** requires it; and (iv) where billing for the tool implicates Rule 1.5. Where none of those triggers is present, blanket disclosure is not mandated. On specificity: the opinion does not prescribe a fixed disclosure script. Informed consent (when required) must be **substantive** — an explanation of the actual risk — and the opinion warns that **boilerplate engagement-letter language is not sufficient** for the self-learning-tool consent. State variation is real (see Q6). *To resolve the MEDIUM:* confirm against (1) the current text of Opinion 512 §§ on Communication and Confidentiality, and (2) the specific state's adoption — Florida (Ethics Op. 24-1) leans toward broader disclosure obligations than the ABA baseline; California's guidance is closer to the ABA's conditional approach.

**Q3.** **[MEDIUM→HIGH]** Yes — attorney review and substantial editing before use is the textbook example of **competent** GAI use under the current state of opinion, *provided* the review is substantive rather than cosmetic. The competent/incompetent line in the drafting context is drawn at **verification of factual and legal assertions**: incompetent use is uncritical reliance on output that is filed or sent without independent confirmation (the *Mata v. Avianca* failure mode — fabricated citations passed through unchecked). Competent use is treating output as a first draft from a capable-but-unreliable junior whose every factual claim, citation, and legal proposition the lawyer independently confirms and owns. Your architecture supports this but does not itself create competence — competence is in the workflow, which is why your "draft — review before use" framing is correct but should be strengthened (see Q41, Q50). Marked MEDIUM only because "substantially edited" is doing a lot of work; the protective fact is *verification*, not edit volume.

## I-B. Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6)

**Q4.** **[MEDIUM]** Transmitting client information to a cloud AI provider's API is best analyzed under the **Rule 1.6(c) "reasonable efforts" standard**, and under prevailing authority it is **generally permissible if reasonable efforts are made**, rather than a per-se prohibited "disclosure." The governing framework predates AI: ABA Formal Opinion 477R (2017) and Comment 18 to Rule 1.6 set out the reasonableness factors — sensitivity of the information, likelihood of disclosure absent safeguards, cost and difficulty of safeguards, and the extent to which safeguards adversely affect the lawyer's ability to represent the client. Cloud vendors (including AI providers on enterprise/API terms) are routinely treated like other technology vendors (cloud storage, e-discovery hosts, practice-management SaaS) whose contractual confidentiality and security controls let the lawyer satisfy "reasonable efforts." The contested part — and why this is MEDIUM, not HIGH — is that Opinion 512's distinct concern is **not transmission per se but the provider's downstream data use** (training on inputs, retention, sub-processor disclosure). Strong contractual protections that disable training and limit retention move the analysis decisively toward the safe harbor; consumer terms that permit training (the *Heppner* facts) move it the other way. *To resolve:* obtain and read the **actual API/enterprise terms** of each provider (Anthropic's commercial terms disable training on API inputs by default; OpenAI's API terms similarly; Google's Vertex/Gemini API terms vary by tier) and confirm against Opinion 512's Confidentiality section.

**Q5.** **[MEDIUM]** Local-first architecture creates a **meaningfully better, but not categorically different,** Rule 1.6 posture than a tool that routes through an intermediate vendor server. It eliminates one whole category of risk — a **Advisor Prep Hero-operated** server that receives, caches, logs, or could be breached/subpoenaed. That is a genuine and defensible advantage, and it removes Advisor Prep Hero itself from the trust chain. **But** be precise about what it does *not* do: the client data still travels to the **AI provider's** API, so the dispositive Rule 1.6 question — what Anthropic/OpenAI/Google do with the data — is **unchanged** by local-first. Local-first solves "does the *tool vendor* see my data" (answer: no), not "does the *AI model provider* see my data" (answer: yes, by design). A sophisticated practitioner will catch this distinction immediately (see Q45(c) and Q52). The honest, strong framing is: *local-first removes the middleman and shrinks the attack/subpoena surface to a single provider whose terms you control via your own account* — not *local-first means your data stays on your machine* (it doesn't, once you make an API call).

Comprehensive; covers competence, confidentiality, supervision, billing, candor.   *To resolve the MEDIUM:* pull the **current** controlling text for each of CA/NY/TX/FL (these are evolving fast), and confirm whether each is a binding rule, a formal ethics opinion, or non-binding "practical guidance." The practical takeaway for product copy: **do not market a single national compliance claim**; California's heightened confidentiality duty and Florida's disclosure posture are the two that most affect your messaging.

**Q7.** **[MEDIUM]** Directing users to "review the provider's terms of service and privacy policies" is **necessary but not by itself sufficient** to discharge the Opinion 512 diligence obligation. Opinion 512 frames the duty as an **affirmative, substantive evaluation** of how the tool uses data — specifically whether inputs are used for training, how long they are retained, who the sub-processors are, and what the breach/disclosure posture is. A pointer that says "go read the terms" leaves the substantive judgment entirely to a user who may not know what to look for. Stronger discharge: Advisor Prep Hero should (a) **summarize the relevant data-handling terms** of each supported provider in plain language at the point of provider selection, (b) **flag the training/retention posture** of the chosen tier, and (c) prompt the user to confirm they are on a **commercial/API tier with training disabled** before any client data is used. That converts a passive disclaimer into an actual diligence aid — which is also a marketing asset. *To resolve:* read the operative paragraphs of Opinion 512 on provider due diligence and confirm the per-provider summaries against current terms.

## I-C. Attorney-client privilege and work product

**Q8.** **[MEDIUM]** Pre-*Heppner*, there was **no controlling case law** on whether transmitting privileged communications to a cloud AI API waives privilege; the analysis ran by analogy. *Heppner* is now the **first on-point authority** (see lead finding). The doctrinal backbone:
- **Third-party disclosure waives privilege** unless the third party is within the privilege — i.e., an **agent necessary to the rendition of legal advice** under *United States v. Kovel*, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961) (accountant retained by counsel held within privilege). Translators, e-discovery vendors, forensic accountants, and consultants have all been brought within privilege on the *Kovel* rationale.
- The AI-vendor question is therefore: **Is the AI provider more like a *Kovel* agent (no waiver) or like a disinterested third party / public posting (waiver)?**
- *Heppner*'s answer, on its facts: a **consumer** tool, used **without counsel's direction**, under terms permitting training and disclosure, is **not** a *Kovel* agent → waiver. But the opinion's dicta keep the *Kovel* door open for **counsel-directed, confidentiality-preserving** use.
  *To resolve the MEDIUM:* (1) read the *Heppner* memorandum opinion in full (Dkt. No. 27) rather than relying on firm summaries; (2) check for any post-*Heppner* decisions or appeals; (3) consult a current privilege treatise — Rice, *Attorney-Client Privilege in the United States*, and the *Wigmore on Evidence* / *Paul Rice* treatment of the *Kovel* agent doctrine — for how courts are likely to extend it to AI vendors.

**Q9.** **[MEDIUM]** Yes — *Heppner* confirms all three of your variables move the analysis:
- **(a) Contractual confidentiality protections:** central. The court keyed on the **consumer terms** that permitted training/retention/disclosure. Enterprise/API terms that disable training and limit retention are the strongest single fact cutting against waiver, because they support a *reasonable expectation of confidentiality*.
- **(b) On-machine processing vs. transmission to provider servers:** matters, but note the limit — once data hits the **provider's** API it has left the user's machine regardless of Advisor Prep Hero's local-first design. The relevant "on-machine vs. off" line for privilege is whether the **model provider** receives it, not whether **Advisor Prep Hero** does. (This is the same distinction as Q5.)
- **(c) Affirmative exclusion of privileged content from prompts:** the cleanest protection of all. If privileged communications never enter the prompt, there is nothing to waive. Workflow design that helps the user **scope what goes into a prompt** is therefore a privilege feature, not just a UX nicety (see Q49 on matter isolation).
  *To resolve:* same as Q8 — full opinion + treatise + provider-terms verification.

**Q10.** **[HIGH] — Definitive answer: the case is REAL.** See the lead finding for the full citation, facts, holding, and reasoning. To restate the four required points your document asked for:
1. **Does it exist?** Yes. *United States v. Heppner*, No. 1:25-cr-00503-JSR (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026), Dkt. No. 27 (Rakoff, J.).
2. **Is the reported holding accurate?** **Partially — and your phrasing needs correction.** Your appendix described it as holding "that a party's use of a consumer AI tool with documents at issue in litigation constituted a waiver of privilege over those documents." More precisely: (a) the disputed materials were **AI-generated documents the defendant created**, not pre-existing privileged documents he fed in; (b) the holding is better stated as the AI documents **were not privileged in the first place** (Claude is not a lawyer; no attorney-client communication; created without counsel's direction) **and**, to the extent any privileged input was involved, **confidentiality was waived** by use of a consumer tool with training-permissive terms; (c) it was a **non-lawyer client**, not an attorney using a practice tool. The "consumer AI use can defeat privilege" gist is correct; the mechanical "waiver over documents at issue" framing is imprecise.
3. **Why couldn't you verify it earlier?** It was decided **after most AI training cutoffs** (Feb 2026). AI models with knowledge ending in late 2025 / early 2026 would have **no record** of it and could easily have told you it appeared fabricated — a useful object lesson in why post-cutoff citations must be checked against live sources (CourtListener docket 71872024; the opinion is Dkt. No. 27).
4. **Can you use it?** Yes, but **only with the precise characterization above**, and framed as favorable-but-distinguishable rather than as a holding that AI destroys privilege. Misstating it as "any AI use waives privilege" would be both inaccurate and, ironically, bad for your product. See the suggested language in the lead finding.

**Q11.** **[MEDIUM]** Work product (Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3); *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947)) applies **differently** from privilege, and generally **more protectively**, on the waiver question. Key differences:
- **Different waiver standard.** Privilege is waived by disclosure to *any* outside-the-privilege third party. Work product is waived only by disclosure that **substantially increases the likelihood that an adversary will obtain the material** — disclosure to a non-adversary third party who shares an interest in confidentiality typically does **not** waive it.
- **Implication for AI transmission:** sending attorney work product to a provider on confidentiality-preserving terms is *less* likely to waive than sending privileged communications, because the provider is not an adversary and the terms reduce the likelihood the adversary obtains it.
- **But** *Heppner* still found **no work-product protection** — because the materials were not prepared **at counsel's direction** and did not reveal counsel's strategy. The doctrinal lesson: work-product protection is strongest when (i) counsel directs the AI use and (ii) the prompts/outputs reflect counsel's mental impressions — which then raises the *opposite* risk that the **prompt itself is highly protected work product** you do not want exposed (see Q22).
  *To resolve the MEDIUM:* confirm the *substantial-increase* waiver standard in the controlling circuit (it is the majority federal rule but articulated variously); FRE 502(b) inadvertent-disclosure protection and 502(d) clawback orders interact here.

**Q12.** **[MEDIUM]** A locally stored Advisor Prep Hero audit/conversation log **is potentially discoverable**, and the answer turns on **content**, exactly as you suspect:
- **Pure procedural metadata** (timestamp, model name, output filename, no substantive content): discoverable as a "document" under FRCP 34 if relevant, but typically low-sensitivity and unlikely to reveal anything protected. Often the kind of thing produced or logged without privilege concern.
- **Logs containing the prompts and AI responses:** these may contain **privileged communications** (if they reproduce attorney-client communications), **work product** (if they reflect the attorney's mental impressions and litigation strategy — and a strategy-laden prompt is core opinion work product under Rule 26(b)(3)), or both. Those are protectable, but the protection must be **asserted and logged** (privilege log), and is **subject to waiver analysis** if the underlying use already waived it (the *Heppner* trap).
- The mere existence of an append-only local log does not by itself create discovery exposure that wouldn't already exist for the workspace files; it is the **substance** that matters.
  *Product implication:* the safest design keeps the **metadata log** (defensible, low-risk, supports the Rule 5.3 supervision story — Q47) clearly **separable** from substantive content, so a litigant can produce/justify the metadata without dragging in protected material. *To resolve:* circuit-specific FRCP 34 scope and FRE 502 clawback practice; confirm with a litigator how local AI logs are being treated in current discovery practice.

## I-D. Supervision of non-lawyer assistance (Rules 5.1, 5.3)

**Q13.** **[HIGH]** Yes — the analogy is **explicitly endorsed**. ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies Rules 5.1 and 5.3 to GAI directly, and the comment to Rule 5.3 was retitled in spirit to cover "nonlawyer **assistance**" (not just "assistants") precisely to reach outsourced and technological help. The supervisory obligation does **not** require a full independent re-draft from scratch; it requires **managerial measures** (firm-level policies, vetting of tools, training) plus **case-level review** sufficient to catch the tool's characteristic failure modes. In practice that means **substantive review of each material assertion** the lawyer will rely on or hold out as true — particularly citations, factual claims, and legal propositions — not a line-by-line rewrite. The standard is the same "reasonable efforts to ensure conduct compatible with the lawyer's obligations" that governs a supervised paralegal. *Authority:* Opinion 512 (Supervision section); Rules 5.1, 5.3 and Comments.

**Q14.** **[MEDIUM]** Yes — relying on the Deposition Contradiction Finder's flagged inconsistencies **without independently cross-checking each flagged passage against the transcript** would likely be a supervisory failure under the Rule 5.3 analogy, and would compound into a Rule 1.1 competence problem and (if used in a filing) a Rule 3.3 candor problem. A disciplinary panel would apply a **reasonableness** standard: did the lawyer take reasonable steps to verify the tool's output before relying on it? Because deposition contradiction-finding is exactly the kind of task where GAI **hallucinates plausible-but-false** matches (misattributing testimony, inventing a contradiction, missing a real one), Opinion 512's "task-specific verification" principle puts this near the **high-verification** end of the spectrum. The protective practice — which your template should *enforce*, not merely suggest — is to make every flagged item **link back to the exact transcript citation** so verification is one click, and to label the output "candidate inconsistencies requiring source verification." *To resolve:* the MEDIUM is about how a *specific* state's disciplinary body would frame the standard; the direction (verification required) is settled.

## I-E. Safekeeping of client property (Rule 1.15)

**Q15.** **[MEDIUM]** Ingesting client documents into Advisor Prep Hero for analysis is primarily a **Rule 1.6 (confidentiality/security) question, not a core Rule 1.15 (property) question**, but 1.15 is implicated at the margins. Rule 1.15 governs safeguarding client *property* in the lawyer's possession (originals, files, and in many jurisdictions client data). The obligations it adds beyond ordinary digital security are: (i) **don't lose or destroy** the client's originals/files, (ii) **return** them on request/at end of representation, and (iii) keep them **identifiable and segregated** from the lawyer's own property. Creating a **local AI-generated analysis** of those documents is generally **lawyer work product**, not client property, so it doesn't itself expand 1.15 duties — but it does expand **1.6** duties (now there's a second copy/derivative of sensitive material to secure and, eventually, dispose of). The practical 1.15 hooks for your product: **don't alter or risk the integrity of the originals** during ingestion (read-only handling), and support **clean deletion/return** workflows. *To resolve:* whether a given state treats electronic client data as "property" under 1.15 versus solely under 1.6 — a handful of states and ethics opinions address this; confirm for CA/NY/TX/FL.

**Q18.** **[MEDIUM]** The malpractice exposure for an AI-origin error that the lawyer fails to catch and that causes client harm is **the same legal exposure as any other negligent error** — the AI origin neither creates a new tort nor excuses the lawyer. Elements: duty, breach (failure to meet the standard of care), causation, damages. The standard of care is what a **reasonably competent attorney** would do; under Opinion 512 and Rule 1.1, that now includes **verifying AI output**. So the AI origin tends to **harden, not soften,** the breach analysis: "I relied on the AI" is not a defense; it may be evidence of the breach (failure to supervise/verify). Conversely, **documented verification workflow** (which your audit log and "review before use" gating support) is evidence the lawyer *met* the standard of care — a genuine risk-management value proposition. *To resolve the MEDIUM:* state-specific legal-malpractice standard-of-care articulation and any AI-specific gloss; have a malpractice-defense attorney confirm how the AI-assisted origin is being argued in current cases.

## I-G. Patent-specific issues (absolute novelty)

**Q19.** **[MEDIUM]** Your current understanding is **substantially correct**. The governing principle in absolute-novelty jurisdictions (Europe: EPC Art. 54 — an invention is new if not "made available to the public") is that the disclosure must be **public** — available to a member of the public free of any obligation of confidence. A transmission **under an enforceable obligation of confidentiality** is generally **not** a novelty-destroying public disclosure. Nuances that make this MEDIUM rather than HIGH:
- **The confidentiality must be real and provable.** The protection rests on the recipient being bound to confidence. So the **provider's terms must actually impose confidentiality** (again, the consumer-vs-enterprise distinction bites: consumer terms that permit training/disclosure could undermine the "obligation of confidence" argument).
- **US is different and more forgiving:** post-AIA 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1) gives a **one-year grace period** for the inventor's own disclosures — but you should *not* rely on the grace period as a design assumption because (a) it doesn't help in most foreign jurisdictions and (b) it's a safety net, not a license to disclose.
- **Jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction:** EPC states and **China** are strict absolute-novelty regimes (China has narrow grace exceptions); **Japan** and **Korea** have limited grace periods (6–12 months) with formalities. The risk is real enough that **"be careful" is the correct framing, not "safe with cloud AI under contract."**
  *To resolve the MEDIUM:* a patent attorney should confirm (1) that each supported provider's operative terms create a legally sufficient confidentiality obligation for novelty purposes, and (2) the current grace-period rules for any target foreign jurisdictions. The defensible product message: *don't put unfiled invention details into any AI tool unless you've confirmed the tool's terms impose confidentiality and you've spoken to patent counsel.*

**Q20.** **[MEDIUM]** Using AI to process a **pre-filing** invention disclosure carries a **distinct, real risk** precisely because it is pre-filing: the absolute-novelty bar (Q19) is live until a priority filing exists. The specific danger is not the existence of the template but **what tier/tool the user runs it on**. If the user runs the Patent Disclosure Draft on a **consumer** tool with training-permissive terms, you have arguably created exactly the "disclosure to a third party not under confidence" event that could be argued to defeat novelty in a strict jurisdiction — a catastrophic, irreversible client harm. Mitigations the template should **enforce**: (i) a hard gate confirming the user is on a **confidentiality-preserving provider tier** before any invention detail is entered; (ii) a prominent warning that this content is **novelty-sensitive** and should not be processed by any tool whose terms permit retention/training/disclosure; (iii) a recommendation to **file a provisional first** where timing allows. *To resolve:* patent counsel confirmation of the confidentiality-sufficiency of each provider's terms; this is the single highest-irreversibility risk in your legal pack and deserves the strongest gating.

## I-H. Discovery implications

**Q21.** **[MEDIUM]** The metadata audit log (timestamp, model, prompt **summary**, output filename) is **a "document" within FRCP 34** (which expressly reaches electronically stored information) and is therefore **discoverable if relevant and not protected**. Work-product protection is **unlikely to attach to bare metadata** because metadata generally does not reveal the attorney's mental impressions or litigation strategy — it's procedural. The risk concentrates in the **"prompt summary"** field: if the summary captures substantive strategy, it inherits the protected character (and the discovery exposure) of the prompt itself. ## II-A. IRC § 7216 — scope and application

**Q23.** **[MEDIUM]** Your framing — **"an open question worth thinking through," not "a confirmed statutory violation"** — is **accurate and defensible.** There is, to my knowledge, **no IRS ruling, PLR, or published enforcement action** applying § 7216 specifically to AI API calls, so the question is genuinely unsettled. **Q24.** **[MEDIUM]** The multi-provider problem is real, and the governing detail is **Rev. Proc. 2013-14** (which modified and superseded Rev. Proc. 2008-35) for **Form 1040-series** taxpayers under Treas. Reg. § 301.7216-3. Key constraints your template must respect:
- A consent to **disclose** and a consent to **use** **cannot be combined in one document**; multiple disclosures or multiple uses must be **separately and specifically identified** (Treas. Reg. § 301.7216-3(c); Rev. Proc. 2013-14 §§ 5.01, 6.02).

**Q25.** **[MEDIUM]** § 7216's scope — "information furnished … for, or in connection with, the preparation" — is **broad**, and your three sub-questions break down:

**Q26.** **[MEDIUM]** Your "criminal misdemeanor" characterization is **technically accurate but practically misleading**, and you should adjust it.

**Q27.** **[MEDIUM]** Yes — the analysis differs for an attorney-preparer, but **§ 7216 itself applies regardless of professional status** (it reaches any "tax return preparer"). The differences:
