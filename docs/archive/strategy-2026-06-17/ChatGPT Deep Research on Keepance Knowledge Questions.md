**Q8.**  Under Model Rule 1.6, attorneys *may* (but need not) disclose client confidences in limited exceptions (e.g. to prevent death or serious bodily harm, or to prevent certain frauds involving the lawyer’s services).  These are permissive exceptions only.  No “voluntary disclosure rule” compels disclosure except where a statute or court order applies.  In short, unless one of the narrow 1.6(b) exceptions is met, confidentiality continues; even under an exception, disclosure is discretionary.  (Confidence: **HIGH**)

**Q9.**  The permissive exceptions in Rule 1.6(b) apply only to very serious threats.  For example, if a client is planning a violent crime or large fraud that threatens substantial injury, an attorney may reveal what is necessary to prevent it.  If the conduct is merely nonviolent or minor (e.g. low-level tax evasion or misconduct), 1.6(b) generally offers no exception.  In short, only if the client’s actions meet the rule’s threshold (e.g. “reasonably certain” death or significant economic harm) could disclosure be permitted; otherwise the answer is the same as Q8.  (Confidence: **MEDIUM**) *Further check:* Exact criteria for “reasonably certain” crime/fraud vary, so see model-comment or local ethics guidance.

**Q10.**  *U.S. v. Heppner* (S.D.N.Y. No. 25-cr-503 (JSR), Feb. 17, 2026) is a real case. **Q11.**  The work-product doctrine (Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3)) protects materials prepared by or at the direction of counsel in anticipation of litigation.  AI-generated drafts can be work product if they were created under the lawyer’s direction for the case.  As K&L Gates observes, *“GenAI data created at counsel’s direction to analyze claims, defenses, or litigation strategy, which may qualify as work product”*【76†L123-L132】.  However, if the AI output was generated independently (e.g. not directed by lawyer, or for business purposes), it likely is not protected.  In short: if an attorney uses the AI tool as part of preparing the case (under counsel’s direction), the output can be treated as work product; if not, it probably isn’t【76†L123-L132】.  (Confidence: **MEDIUM**)

**Q12.**  If the attorney uses Advisor Prep Hero (with conversation stored locally) to draft a document at the lawyer’s direction for a client’s case, the AI output is very likely work-product. **Q22.**  If Advisor Prep Hero’s AI runs entirely locally with no data sent to the cloud, §7216(a) is not triggered.  The statute forbids a preparer from disclosing or using information *furnished* to him for preparing returns, except to prepare the return.  Here, the attorney is both the preparer and the sole holder of the data, so nothing is “furnished” to another preparer.  Using the AI for legitimate preparation purposes would fall under the exception “to prepare” the return【28†L46-L54】.  In short, no outside disclosure occurs, and using the tool to prepare the client’s return is exactly the permitted use.  (Confidence: **HIGH**)

**Q23.**  Section 7216 applies to *any* person preparing federal returns (including lawyers who prepare or assist in tax returns for compensation)【28†L46-L54】.  A tax attorney using Advisor Prep Hero as part of return preparation is a “tax return preparer” under 7216.  The local vs. cloud architecture does not change the status: if the attorney is doing return prep, 7216 applies.  (Confidence: **HIGH**)

**Q24.**  IRS regulations (Treas. Reg. 301.7216-3) set strict consent requirements.  Consent must be *written or electronic* and signed【34†L82-L90】.  It must identify the preparer and taxpayer by name and tax ID【34†L122-L131】, describe the specific information to be disclosed or used【34†L141-L144】, and explain the purpose (e.g. to a particular service provider)【34†L124-L131】.  The consent must also state it is given voluntarily.  In short, the consent must explicitly name parties, data, and purposes【34†L122-L131】【34†L141-L144】.  (Confidence: **HIGH**)

**Q25.**  The phrase “furnished to him for, or in connection with, the preparation of any such return” refers to any information given by the taxpayer (directly or indirectly) for use in preparing the return【28†L46-L54】.  If the client provides the attorney with documents or data to prepare the tax return, that information is “furnished” to the preparer under §7216【28†L46-L54】.  Thus all client-provided tax data falls within 7216’s scope from the moment it is furnished for preparation.  (Confidence: **HIGH**)

**Q26.**  The “furnishing” analysis depends on whether client data is actually disclosed to another person.
