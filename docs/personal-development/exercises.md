# Hands-On Exercises (do these, don't just read)

Reading is the warm-up. These five exercises are where the real progress happens. They
are ordered. If you only did the first three, you'd be ahead of most founders. Each one
is sized to fit one or two of your uninterrupted weekend blocks.

> **Why these matter more than the books:** you already have something most founders would
> kill for — a real financial advisory firm (your wife's) you can learn from up close.
> These exercises turn that access into a product spec, your first customer conversations,
> and a clear story. The books make you better at these exercises; the exercises are what
> actually move Keepance forward.

---

## Exercise 1 — Shadow your wife's firm and map how documents flow
*(One weekday or a focused Saturday at the firm · free · do this FIRST)*

Sit beside an advisor and their assistant for a real working block. Your job is to watch,
not pitch. Capture, in a simple diagram, every place a client document is **created,
received, named, stored, and found again** — custodian statements, signed forms, financial
plans, meeting notes, emails.

Then trace the **compliance** angle (this is the heart of why Keepance exists):
- Where do they keep the records the SEC requires them to keep for years?
- If a regulator asked for a specific client email from two years ago, how would they find it?
- Who can open client files, and what's their plan if data leaks? (This is the new
  Reg S-P rule — see Library 2.)

**Output:** a one-page "current-state map" with the 3-5 worst friction points circled.
That single page is simultaneously your product spec, your competitive baseline, and your
first customer-discovery interview.

## Exercise 2 — Inventory their software stack and find "where the knowledge lives"
*(2-3 hours at the firm · free)*

List every tool the firm pays for (CRM, planning software, custodian portal, file storage,
email). For each, write down what it's the official home for, and where its files actually
sit. Then ask the killer question:

> *"When a client calls, where do you look first — and what's never written down anywhere?"*

That gap — the knowledge that only exists in someone's head or buried in old email — is
exactly the "institutional memory" Keepance claims to be.

**Output:** a stack inventory plus a short list of knowledge that lives only in people's
heads. This tells you precisely what Keepance must capture to be indispensable rather than
just another folder.

## Exercise 3 — Run a 10-interview discovery sprint
*(Spread across 2-3 weekends · free · read* The Mom Test *and* Talking to Humans *first)*

This is the highest-leverage skill you can build. The trap (from *The Mom Test*): people
lie to be polite. So you ask about their *real life and past behavior*, never about your idea.

1. Write a one-page interview guide. **Rule: zero questions about Keepance.** Ask only about
   *their* world. Good questions:
   - "Walk me through the last time a client asked you something and you couldn't find the
     answer fast."
   - "What's the messiest part of keeping track of what you know about a client?"
   - "When you onboard a new client, what falls through the cracks?"
2. Have your wife introduce you to 8-10 advisors (her firm, plus peers from communities like
   XYPN or NAPFA — see Library 1). Take notes, or record with permission.
3. Listen for **commitment signals**: have they already paid for a workaround, built a
   spreadsheet, or lost a client over this? Those are the real, painful problems.

**Output:** a one-page list of the top 3 recurring problems, ranked by how much pain and
money advisors already throw at them. *That list — not your current feature set — should
steer the launch.*

## Exercise 4 — Position Keepance and write its one-page story
*(One weekend · free · read* Obviously Awesome *and Andy Raskin's sales-deck essay first)*

After the interviews, you'll finally have the raw material to answer "what *is* Keepance?"

1. **Positioning** (from Dunford's canvas):
   - What do advisors use *today* instead? (Probably a CRM plus folders plus memory — or
     "nothing, we just wing it.")
   - What does Keepance do meaningfully better?
   - Exactly which advisor cares most? (Likely a small fee-only firm worried about the new
     compliance rules — like your wife's.)
   - What category should they file you under?
2. **Story** (from Raskin's 5 beats): the big shift hitting advisors (rising compliance
   pressure + scattered client knowledge) → who wins vs. loses → the promised land (an
   advisor who can answer any client or compliance question in seconds) → Keepance as the
   bridge → one piece of proof.
3. **The one metric that matters right now:** pre-launch, it is *not* revenue. Pick a single
   demand signal you can move this quarter — for example, **the number of real advisors who
   finished a discovery call and explicitly said "I'd pay for this" or agreed to be a design
   partner.** Write it down, set a target, make it your scoreboard.

**Output:** one page. If you can't fill a box, that's not writer's block — it's a research
gap that tells you what to go ask advisors next. This page becomes the spine of your sales
deck, your homepage, and your raise-money-or-not decision.

Drop the phrase "private client intelligence layer" from anything an advisor will read,
*unless* your interviews prove advisors actually say it back to you. Use the plain sentence
they'd repeat to a peer instead.

## Exercise 5 — The reverse-demo test
*(One weekend · free · do after you have a rough demo)*

Using only fake or anonymized data, build one sample client household inside Keepance. Then
sit your wife (or a friendly advisor) in front of it and ask five blunt questions:

- "Would this actually help you prepare for a client review?"
- "What's missing?"
- "What would your compliance person object to?"
- "What would you *never* trust this app to do?"
- "What would make you actually pay for it?"

**Output:** 5 must-fix gaps in the demo, 5 words or claims to delete from your pitch, and 5
workflows worth building next. Watching a real advisor poke at it for 20 minutes will teach you
more than any amount of guessing.

## Exercise 6 — The paid design-partner ask
*(After ~10 discovery calls · the most important test of all)*

This is the one most founders flinch at, and it's the one that matters. Compliments are not
traction. The only honest proof that you're building something sellable is someone willing to
*pay*. After your discovery sprint, pick the 3 warmest advisors, show them the demo, and make a
real ask:

> "Would you be open to a 90-day design-partner pilot — I onboard one real client household with
> you, we meet weekly, and I shape the product around your workflow?"

Put a price on it: **$500-$1,500 for the 90 days, credited toward their first year** if they
continue. The money is almost beside the point; the *yes* (or a precise "I'd pay if it did X") is
the signal you're hunting for.

**Output:** one of two things — an advisor who agrees to pay, or a crystal-clear statement of the
exact missing piece (usually a trust or compliance artifact) that's blocking the yes. Both are
gold. A vague "looks great, keep me posted" is a polite no, and you treat it as one.

## Exercise 7 — Set your weekly weekend operating rhythm
*(10 minutes to set up, then run it every weekend block · free)*

A simple routine so your blocks get used well and aimless YouTube has nowhere to sneak in.
Put it on a sticky note.

**Friday night — 10-minute setup (the most important 10 minutes):**
1. Write down **ONE outcome** for the weekend block. One, not five.
2. Break it into **3 concrete first tasks** you could start cold.
3. **Pre-commit against YouTube:** log out of it, put your phone in another room, or add a
   site blocker. Make the work the path of least resistance.

**During the block — 90/15 sprints:**
4. Work **90 minutes on the top task, one window only.** Then a real **15-minute break** —
   walk, water, look outside, *not* a screen. Repeat 3-4 times.
5. **One movement break mid-day:** a 20-30 minute brisk walk or workout. It's not a break
   *from* the work — it's fuel for the next sprint.

**End of the block — 5-minute close:**
6. Write **what got done** and the **single next action** for next time, so you start
   instantly instead of drifting.

**Weekly guardrail:** keep a consistent wake time even on weekends, and get sunlight within
an hour of waking. That's what keeps the *next* block sharp instead of foggy.

Run it for four weekends before judging it. The setup and the close are what kill the
YouTube drift, because most aimless scrolling happens in the undefined gaps — and this
removes the gaps.
