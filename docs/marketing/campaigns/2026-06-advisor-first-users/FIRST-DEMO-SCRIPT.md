# The first-demo script (repeatable)

*A clean runbook for the 4-step demo you can re-run for advisor #2, #3, #4. Internal, for Jameson to drive. The wife-specific reading guide ("she loves you, so here's how to get honest answers") lives in `docs/strategy/2026-06-25-proof-sprint/01-design-partner-session-plan.md` and isn't repeated here. This is the part you reuse for everyone.*

The whole demo is about 15 minutes. You drive it, but you watch more than you perform. The goal of every demo is the same: get them to one concrete commitment, which is to put a real client into Keepance and use it before a real meeting within about a week. A "wow, cool" with no next step is a polite failure, not a win.

---

## Before you start (the prep checklist)

- [ ] Run the **polished build**, not the developer one. Confirm with the build session which is which.
- [ ] **Disconnect the test email account** so nothing off-topic shows up on screen.
- [ ] **Clear the clutter:** old questions, old recent-workspaces, anything left over from the law-firm version. The screen should look advisor-native and clean.
- [ ] **Load one believable client.** The Hendricks sample household works. Far stronger: ask the advisor ahead of time if you can use one of their own real clients, with permission. A real client makes the whole thing land.
- [ ] **Run the full 4 steps yourself once, start to finish, the day before.** Confirm the Client Map builds and the answer shows its sources. Those are the parts that broke recently.
- [ ] If it's their real client, ask them to **pick one they're meeting soon**, so the demo has a real target.

---

## The 4 steps (drive, then stop and watch)

After each step, stop. Let them react. Watch their face. Note where they lean in, where they look lost, and what they say without being asked.

### Step 1 — Sign in, pick a folder and an AI option

Say:
> This runs on your own computer. You pick a folder of client files, and you pick how the AI runs: either fully on your machine, or with your own key so it goes straight to Anthropic or OpenAI on your account. Either way, nothing routes through me. I never see any of it.

**Watch:** does the "stays on your machine" idea land, or do they not care? That tells you how much the privacy story is doing for this person.

### Step 2 — Import the client's pile of files

Say:
> So here's the mess. The statements, the plan, the tax doc, the email thread. I'm just pointing it at all of it at once.

**Watch:** do they recognize this as their actual life? You want a flicker of "yep, that's my Tuesday."

### Step 3 — The Client Map builds itself

This is the wow moment. Let it happen and stay quiet.

Say (then stop talking):
> And it reads across all of it and builds one picture of the household. Goals, accounts, what's open, what changed.

**Watch:** where does their eye go first? Do they say something like "oh, that'd save me time"? Silence from you here is a feature.

### Step 4 — Ask a real question, then click the source

Say:
> Now watch this part. I'll ask it something I'd actually want before a meeting.

Ask a real pre-meeting question out loud, like "what's still open before this review?" Then, when the answer comes back, click one of its sources.

> And every answer shows its work. See, this came from this file, this date, this line. You're not trusting it blind, you're checking it.

**Watch:** does the cited-source thing matter to them? For a fiduciary it usually does, and watching them realize it is the best signal in the whole demo.

---

## Then hand them the keyboard

This is the most important ten minutes, and it's the step people skip.

Say:
> Now you try. Ask it something about this client you'd actually want to know before a meeting.

Then give them the mouse and go quiet. Let them struggle a little. Note every single place they get stuck. Watching a real advisor drive it themselves tells you more than an hour of you talking.

---

## The questions to ask them (and the ones never to)

**Ask these.** They're about real behavior, so the answers are trustworthy:
- "Walk me through how you prep for a client meeting today."
- "When did you last waste time hunting for something in a client file?"
- "If you used this for real, whose files would you point it at first?"
- "What would your compliance person want to know before you could use it?"
- "What would stop you, or your firm, from actually using it?"
- "Who else, besides you, would have to say yes?"

**Never ask these.** They invite polite, useless answers:
- "Do you like it?"
- "Would you use this?" (hypothetical, people say yes to be nice)
- "Would you pay for this?" (hypothetical, watch for a real commitment instead)

---

## Reading the result

**Green (it's working):**
- They ask the price without you bringing it up.
- They ask "can I use this on my real clients now?"
- They want to show a specific colleague.
- They get frustrated when something's slow. Frustration means they care.
- They follow up the next day, unprompted.

**Red (be honest with yourself):**
- Warm and encouraging, but no specific action.
- "This is cool" with no real moment from their own work where it would've helped.
- They can't name a single time this would have saved them.

---

## The ask at the end

Don't settle for enthusiasm. Go for one small, concrete commitment:

> Would you be up for putting one real client in this week and using it to prep for an actual meeting? Then just tell me what worked and what was wrong.

If they say yes, you have a real user. If they won't, that's the most useful signal of all, and you want to understand exactly why (too clunky? a compliance worry? not actually a real pain?).

---

## Right after (don't skip)

- **Write your notes within the hour,** while it's fresh. Use the template in the session plan.
- **Pick the single most important thing to fix** before the next advisor sees it.
- **Schedule the next touch:** when they'll try it on a real client.
- If they're at a firm, this is also your scout: ask how the firm buys software, who the compliance gatekeeper is, and for one warm intro to a colleague. One good intro beats fifty cold emails.
