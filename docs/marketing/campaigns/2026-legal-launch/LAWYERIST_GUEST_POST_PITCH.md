**Estimated length:** 1,000-1,200 words
**Audience:** Solo and small-firm practitioners using AI tools right now, or thinking about it

**The angle:**

ABA Formal Opinion 512 gave practitioners the first formal guidance on AI use in July 2024. Most of the coverage at the time focused on what the opinion permitted. I want to write about what it implicitly flagged: that the confidentiality analysis isn't just about a subscription's terms of service, it's about where the data actually goes.

The piece would walk through what happens technically when an attorney pastes a document into a cloud AI tool, what major providers' Terms of Service actually say (not what the sales pitch says), and what practitioners need to evaluate before they can honestly say they've done a competent confidentiality review under Opinion 512.

The second half would cover local-first AI tools: how they work technically, why they sidestep the confidentiality analysis entirely, and what the tradeoffs are. I'm the developer of Advisor Prep Hero, which is one of those tools, and I'll be transparent about that. But the article would be genuinely informational, not a product pitch. Practitioners should walk away knowing what questions to ask about any AI tool, not just mine.

**Why me:**

I'm not an attorney, which is actually useful here. The technical explanation of what happens when data leaves your machine is the part that practitioners often don't get from legal writers who also can't explain it from first principles. I'll be upfront about my perspective and limits.

## Full article draft

*(Write this after the attorney advisor reviews the templates and confirms the framing. Do not submit before then. The pitch above goes first.)*

Working title: "The quiet privilege problem with cloud AI tools"

The article structure:

**Opening (150 words)**
Hook: a concrete scenario. An attorney researching a defense strategy, pasting excerpts from a client file into ChatGPT to summarize. Completely routine in 2026. Then the question: did they just share privileged information with a third party?

**Section 1: What ABA Opinion 512 actually says (200 words)**
Short, accurate summary. Not alarmist. The opinion is actually pretty practical. What it requires: competence, confidentiality review, supervision. What "confidentiality review" actually entails when applied to a cloud AI tool.

**Section 2: Where the data goes (250 words)**
Technical explanation at a practical level. What "cloud AI" means in terms of data flow. Side-by-side of two major providers' current data-use terms (cite specific clauses). Not scary, just accurate. A competent review means knowing this.

**Section 3: The local-first alternative (300 words)**
What local-first tools are. How they work. The data flow: your machine to the provider directly, nothing in the middle. Disclose that I built one. Make clear this is one category of tool among others that solve the problem differently (enterprise agreements, on-prem deployments).

**Section 4: The patent angle (100 words)**
Brief. For IP practitioners specifically, EU absolute-novelty rule and why the local-first analysis matters even more in prosecution contexts.

**Closing (150 words)**
Not a verdict on cloud AI. Just: practitioners who understand what's actually happening will make better decisions and will be able to document that they made a competent choice. The goal is clarity, not fear.
