What else could I be doing with AI to push Keepance forward?

A research note · 2026-06-20 · grounded in our own setup + an independent Codex review

Bottom line — the 3 highest-leverage moves

  1. Make AI test the real app like a lawyer, every day. Point it at the Windows machine to run the same "lawyer demo" start-to-finish and report what's broken — in plain English, with screenshots. This attacks your #1 product risk: it isn't finished on the platform users actually run.
  2. Build a fake litigation case file. One realistic (but invented) set of pleadings, depositions, emails and scanned PDFs that becomes both your testing yardstick and your sales demo — so you never need a real client's files to show it off.
  3. Run an AI "lead-finding engine," not a content firehose. Have AI find 25–50 specific solo/small-firm litigation lawyers a week, with a one-line reason each and a ready first email — you just review the best ones.

You already use AI for a lot — building and reviewing code (Claude + Codex), finding bugs by driving the app, writing marketing copy, and competitive research. So this note focuses on what you're not yet doing. The key shift in mindset: AI is a tireless workforce; the real limit is your hours and your judgment, not how much AI labor you can spend. The best uses below take work off your plate and only ask you for the decisions a human must make.

1 · Finish the product faster

Recent Windows testing showed the app isn't actually done. AI can close that gap without you reading code.

Daily "lawyer demo path" test on real Windowsdo now

Have AI install and drive the real Windows app through the exact path a lawyer would: open a matter → import documents → ask a question → click a citation → make a Word redline → sync email → export a report. Every failure comes back as a plain-English bug report with a screenshot, not a log.

Setup: your Legion laptop as a Windows "test bench" (already started), reached over your private network, with Claude or Codex driving it. Use fast unsigned builds for this — save the hour-long signed build for real releases.

Screenshot → bug brief intakedo now

When you spot something wrong, just drop a screenshot on the server. AI turns it into "what broke, why it matters to a lawyer, how to reproduce it, how serious it is," then hands it to Claude/Codex to fix — only after the problem is reproduced. This plays exactly to your strength: you can judge quality by eye; AI does the translating.

2 · The fake case file (your proving ground)

One invented litigation workspacedo now

AI builds a believable but fake case: pleadings, deposition excerpts, emails, a couple of scanned PDFs, a privileged document, a planted contradiction, a missing piece of evidence. Then every new build is tested against it with the same questions ("What changed in the witness's story?" "Find contradictions." "Draft a privilege log."). It doubles as the demo you show prospects — so the first call never needs their real, confidential files.

3 · Distribution & getting found

This is the real business problem (≈zero users, almost no traffic). AI labor is your unfair advantage here since you have no ad budget and no network.

AI lead-finding enginedo now

Each week, AI finds 25–50 highly specific solo/small-firm litigation lawyers — ideally ones who write or speak about AI, privacy, privilege, discovery, or solo practice. For each: one sentence on why they're a fit, a short tailored first email, and the objection they'll likely raise. You review only the top handful before anything sends.

Setup: AI works through your logged-in Chrome, email, CRM, LinkedIn, bar directories, firm sites, and legal podcasts/newsletters — all tools you already have running.

Public proof, not content volumewhen ready

The highest-value asset isn't another blog post — it's one recorded demo on the fake case, one lawyer-reviewed sample document, and one honest "here's exactly where your data goes" teardown. Save the journalist pitch for after the product is genuinely solid.

4 · Sales copilot

Call briefs, follow-ups, and objection cardswhen selling

Before each demo, AI prepares a one-page brief: who the lawyer is, their practice area, likely pain, what to show, and what not to claim. Afterward it turns your notes into CRM updates and follow-up emails. It also keeps short, current answers to the four objections every legal buyer raises ("Will this waive privilege?", "Where does my data go?", "Why trust a solo founder?", "Does it work with Word?"), each tied to a real screenshot. The single best proof asset: a tailored "Confidentiality Report" for a specific prospect showing what stays local, what goes to their own AI, and what Keepance never sees.

5 · Always-on intelligence

Weekly legal-tech radarset + forget

AI watches competitors, bar opinions, AI-privilege rulings, and Clio/Lexis/Thomson updates, and reports only the changes that affect your pitch, roadmap, or risk. This keeps your claims accurate and gives you timely, credible things to say in outreach.

6 · AI features inside the product

Focus on litigation pain, not generic chat. The strongest candidates, once the basics (cited recall, scanned-PDF reading, email import, Word output) work reliably on Windows:

Matter brief (cited facts of a case) · deposition contradiction finder · discovery gap map (what evidence is missing) · privilege-log draft · one-click "verify this citation" · email-to-matter recall · "what changed since last week?"

What to skip (for now) — likely distractions: daily AI-generated social posts, broad SEO blog spam, sweeping "future of legal AI" essays, Product-Hunt-style launch noise, and big autonomous "agent" features inside the product. None of these move the needle while the product isn't finished and you have no users yet.

Why these, for you specifically

Every recommendation is shaped by three facts about your situation: you're a designer, not an engineer (so AI must translate, not hand you code); you run a server with Claude + Codex + a logged-in browser + email + CRM (so you can spend a lot of AI labor cheaply); and your real bottleneck is finishing the product and reaching lawyers, not generating more code. The moves above turn AI from "a coder" into "a tester, a researcher, a lead-finder, and a sales assistant" — the roles a solo founder usually can't afford.
