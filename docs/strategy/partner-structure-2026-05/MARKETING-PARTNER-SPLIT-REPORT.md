## 1. The Five Models and When Each Fits

There are five structures worth understanding before you choose one. Each makes different assumptions about what your brother is, what the relationship is, and what the exit scenario looks like.

### Model A: Equity Co-Founder (5-20%)

He gets a permanent ownership stake in Advisor Prep Hero, subject to vesting. This is the highest-trust, highest-commitment model. It is appropriate when:
- He is going to own marketing for the next 3-5 years, not just the v2.0 launch
- He is willing to take below-market or zero compensation in the near term
- You are building toward an eventual exit or significant scale where equity has real value
- You want true alignment: his incentives and yours are identical

The downside is that equity is permanent and hard to unwind. If he gives six months and drifts off, you have a cap table problem. Equity without vesting is the single most dangerous structure for a solo bootstrapped founder.

### Model B: Revenue Share Contractor (10-30% of gross revenue)

He gets a percentage of every dollar Advisor Prep Hero earns for as long as the agreement is in force. This requires no equity dilution and is appropriate if he is not quitting his day job or taking on genuine founding-level risk. It is appropriate when:
- His contribution is primarily execution (campaigns, outreach, research) rather than strategy or equity-level ownership
- You want a clean off-ramp if things do not work out
- You are not raising outside capital (investors dislike perpetual revenue share arrangements)

The downside: with Advisor Prep Hero's one-time pricing, his monthly payout is erratic. A quiet month generates almost nothing; a Product Hunt spike generates a windfall. The math is hard to plan around for both parties. This model is covered in depth in Section 8.

### Model C: Hybrid (Small Equity + Revenue Share)

He gets a modest equity stake (5-15%, vested) plus a smaller revenue share (8-15%). This is often the most practical model for bootstrapped indie products. It is appropriate when:
- He needs some near-term income motivation, not just distant equity upside
- You want him on the cap table for alignment purposes but cannot offer full co-founder-level equity
- You expect to convert to subscription or raise a small round eventually

This is the recommended model for Advisor Prep Hero. See Section 11 for specifics.

### Model D: Contractor/Consultant (Cash-Only)

He gets paid market rate per hour, per project, or on retainer. This is purely a commercial arrangement. It is appropriate when:
- You have enough cash to pay real market rates ($75-150/hr, or $2-4K/month on retainer for a realistic marketing consultant)
- You want zero legal entanglement with family
- His involvement is scoped and time-limited, not open-ended

The downside: no skin in the game. Contractors optimize for the scope of work. A co-founder optimizes for the outcome. For marketing, where the value is in sustained, compounding effort, this model often produces the least long-term commitment.

### Model E: Advisor (0.1-1% equity, FAST agreement)

He gets a small options grant governed by the Founder Institute FAST agreement, in exchange for strategic guidance, intros, or occasional review. It is appropriate when:
- His involvement will be light-touch (a few hours per month, occasional review, network access)
- You do not want to give up meaningful equity for a strategic sounding-board role

**For the scope you described** (full ownership of marketing execution, outreach, and research), the advisor model almost certainly underpays him for the work and will not create the incentive alignment you need. The Carta benchmark for advisors is 0.21% median. If he is running your Product Hunt launch, he is not an advisor.

## 2. What Equity Percentage Is Right for Advisor Prep Hero's Stage

The range in research is wide but patterned. Here is how to read it:

| Scenario | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| Pre-revenue, full-time, joining at founding | 20-40% |
| Early revenue, product exists, joining mid-stream, full-time | 10-25% |
| Product live with paying customers, joining post-launch, part-time | 5-15% |
| Advisor, light-touch strategic guidance | 0.1-1% |

**Your specific situation:** Product is live, generating revenue, you have done the full product risk. Your brother is part-time (he presumably has his own job). He is a trusted family member. Based on comparable structures from the Indie Hackers community and general bootstrapped founder practice, the realistic range is **8-15%** if he is treated as a genuine co-founder, or **5-10%** if the structure is more of a senior operator with an equity kicker.

The ICanPitch equity framework puts it this way: a technical co-founder joining 6 months after inception gets 15-25% versus 40-50% at founding. Apply the same discount logic here. You built the entire product before he joined. That prior work has real value that your equity stake already captures.

A useful cross-check: contribution-weighted calculators like Gust's co-founder equity tool and Cake Equity's split guide, when you input "product already built, incoming partner owns marketing only, part-time commitment," consistently output ranges in the 8-15% zone.

**One critical guard:** do not go below 5% if you want genuine co-founder behavior. Research across multiple equity frameworks shows that below 5%, the recipient does not feel psychologically like an owner. They start behaving like a vendor. If the number is below 5%, you are better off using a pure revenue share structure and skipping equity entirely.

## 3. Vesting Schedule Best Practices for a Mid-Stream Co-Founder

The standard in venture-backed startups is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. For a bootstrapped product with a first non-founder hire, this is still the right framework, with two modifications.

**Modification 1: No retroactive credit for him.** Since the product was already built without him, he does not get backdated vesting. His clock starts when the agreement is signed. This is different from how you would treat your own founder equity (where backdating to when you started working is appropriate and recommended).

**Modification 2: Consider a 6-month cliff instead of 12 months.** For a family member in a bootstrapped business, a 12-month cliff can feel punishing if the relationship sours at month 9. A 6-month cliff provides real protection without feeling adversarial.

**What this looks like in practice (10% grant example):**

- Agreement signed: June 2026
- 10% equity grant, 4-year vest, 6-month cliff
- Months 1-6: 0% vests (cliff period)
- Month 6: 12.5% of his grant vests in a lump sum (1.25% of company)
- Months 7-48: Remaining 87.5% vests monthly (~0.18% of company per month)
- If he steps back at month 18: He has vested roughly 32% of his grant (3.2% of company)
- If he completes 4 years: He has earned the full 10%

The vesting schedule is not punitive. It is protective for both of you. If the relationship is going well, vesting accelerates automatically. If it is not, neither party is trapped with an outcome they did not earn.

**One additional mechanism worth considering:** a milestone-accelerated vest. Include a clause that if Advisor Prep Hero crosses a specific revenue threshold (e.g., $50K in total cumulative sales), a bonus percentage vests immediately regardless of time. This rewards marketing outcomes directly, not just time served.

## 4. Tax, Legal, and Entity Implications

This is the area where getting it wrong is expensive and hard to fix. The key variables are your current entity structure and how you document the equity grant.

### Entity Structure

**If Advisor Prep Hero is currently a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC:** Adding a co-founder automatically converts a single-member LLC to a multi-member LLC, which is taxed as a partnership by default. This changes your tax filing from Schedule C to Form 1065, with K-1s issued to each member. This is real administrative overhead. Get a CPA involved before signing anything.

**If you intend to raise outside capital eventually:** A C-Corp (Delaware C-Corp) is the only structure that supports SAFE notes, preferred equity, and QSBS benefits. If Advisor Prep Hero stays bootstrapped forever, this is not urgent. But if there is any chance of outside investment, convert to C-Corp before issuing equity.

**QSBS (Section 1202):** If Advisor Prep Hero is or becomes a C-Corp, both your equity and your brother's equity may qualify for Qualified Small Business Stock treatment, which excludes up to $10M in gains from federal capital gains tax at exit. Requirements: C-Corp structure, holding stock for 5+ years, company had less than $50M in gross assets at time of issuance. At Advisor Prep Hero's current scale, you almost certainly qualify. This is a legitimate long-term tax planning consideration worth discussing with a CPA.

### The 83(b) Election (Critical)

If your brother receives restricted stock (equity subject to vesting), he must file an 83(b) election with the IRS within **30 days of the grant date**. No exceptions. No extensions.

**Without an 83(b) election:** He pays ordinary income tax on the fair market value of each tranche of equity as it vests. If Advisor Prep Hero grows significantly between grant date and vest date, he could face a large tax bill in year 3 or 4 on equity he cannot yet sell.

**With an 83(b) election:** He pays taxes on the total equity at the current (low) value on grant date. At early stage, this is often close to zero. Then when he eventually sells, he pays long-term capital gains rates instead of ordinary income. The rate difference matters: ordinary income up to 37% versus long-term capital gains at 20%.

The IRS released Form 15620 in late 2024 to formalize this process, and began accepting electronic filings in June 2025. This is now easier to execute than historically. The 30-day deadline is still absolute and should be built into the agreement timeline as an explicit action item.

### Revenue Share Tax Treatment

Revenue share payments to your brother are treated as contractor income (1099-NEC if over $600/year). He pays self-employment tax on this income. Simpler than equity from an administrative standpoint, but carries no long-term tax advantages.

**Non-negotiable practical step:** Before executing any equity agreement, spend $500-1,000 on a one-hour consultation with a startup attorney and a CPA who has worked with bootstrapped founders. The cost of getting the entity structure wrong exceeds the cost of advice by an order of magnitude.

## 5. Valuing Your Prior Work: The "Head Start" Discount

There is no universal formula here, but the logic is clear and widely applied.

Your prior contributions to Advisor Prep Hero include: the original product concept, all engineering work, all design work, all initial marketing and validation, the existing customer base, all existing revenue, the brand, and multiple years of personal financial and opportunity-cost risk. This is the baseline asset that your brother is stepping into.

**The Slicing Pie framework** values contributions at fair market rate times a multiplier (2x for non-cash work like time, 4x for cash invested). If you spent two years building a product at even a conservative $75/hr fair market value, working 5-10 hrs/week, that is $39,000-$78,000 in imputed contribution before he joins. Any equity split should reflect that existing foundation.

**The practical implication:** A 12% grant to your brother is not "he gets 12% of what you built." It is "he earns 12% of the future company value by completing four years of vesting." Your existing contributions are already baked into your majority 88% stake. They do not need to be separately valued unless you are formalizing capital accounts in an LLC operating agreement.

**The "head start discount" rule of thumb** from the ICanPitch framework and Stripe's equity guide: a marketing co-founder at inception would command 25-35% of a standard two-founder split. The same person joining after a live product with paying customers exists commands roughly 35-55% of that at-inception value. For Advisor Prep Hero, that math produces a range of roughly 9-17%.

## 6. The Most Common Mistakes When Bringing Family Into a Business

These are documented failure modes from real founder experiences and legal research, not hypotheticals.

**Mistake 1: No written agreement because "it's family."**
The most common failure. Good intentions do not replace documentation. A family relationship makes a legal dispute more expensive emotionally, not less. Write everything down. A $2,000 legal agreement now prevents a $50,000 dispute later.

**Mistake 2: Confusing a loan, a revenue share, and an equity grant.**
These are three completely different legal and tax instruments. If money flows in any direction without documentation, you may accidentally create a partnership, a taxable event, or a gift that triggers gift tax reporting. Be precise in writing.

**Mistake 3: Giving equity before testing fit.**
You need to know if your brother can actually execute marketing for a desktop app before you give him 12% of your company. The 6-month cliff exists exactly for this reason, but you should also structure an explicit trial period before the equity agreement is even signed. See Section 9.

**Mistake 4: Overvaluing the company when setting the grant.**
At a bootstrapped product with modest revenue, the company's equity value is low. A 12% grant at this stage means something very different than a 12% grant after a $500K revenue year. Be honest with each other about what the equity is actually worth today versus in a best-case scenario. Do that math together.

**Mistake 5: Mixing personal and business finances.**
If you lend him cash to cover a marketing spend, document it as a reimbursement, not a loan or contribution. If he buys an ad with his own money and you pay him back, that is an expense reimbursement, not equity contribution. Draw the lines clearly from day one.

**Mistake 6: Not having the "what if it doesn't work" conversation before you start.**
What happens if he steps back at month 8? What if the relationship becomes strained? What if he gets a promotion or new job that consumes his time? These conversations are much easier to have before any documents are signed. Have them explicitly. The specific question to answer together: "If in 18 months neither of us is happy with this arrangement, what does the exit look like?"

**Mistake 7: No role clarity.**
Define precisely what "own marketing" means. Does he have budget authority? Signing authority for tools? Authority to represent Advisor Prep Hero publicly in interviews or communities? Who has final say on messaging? Ambiguity in role definition corrodes co-founder relationships faster than almost anything else.

**Mistake 8: Hiring for availability rather than qualification.**
The research on family businesses is consistent: hiring unqualified family members because they are available and trustworthy rather than because they are the right person leads to resentment and often product/market damage. Be honest with yourself: is your brother the right person for this role, or is he convenient and trustworthy? Those are different questions, and both deserve an honest answer.

## 7. The Co-Founder Agreement: Must-Have Clauses

Whether you structure this as equity or revenue share, the legal agreement needs the following components. Do not use a generic template without having a startup attorney review the final version.

### For an Equity Co-Founder Agreement

**1. Equity percentage and share count.** State the exact percentage and, if shares are issued, the exact number of shares. Vague percentage claims are dangerous if the total share count changes.

**2. Vesting schedule.** All terms in one place: total duration, cliff period, monthly vesting rate after cliff, what happens on voluntary departure (unvested shares revert to company at nominal price), what happens on involuntary termination or death.

**3. IP assignment.** Everything he creates in connection with Advisor Prep Hero marketing — brand assets, copy, campaign materials, research documents, email list ownership, social accounts — belongs to Advisor Prep Hero the company, not to him personally. This clause is non-negotiable and any future investor will require it.

**4. Role definition.** Title (e.g., "Co-Founder, Head of Marketing"), responsibilities, and the explicit scope of decision-making authority.

**5. Decision-making thresholds.** Day-to-day marketing decisions are his. Decisions above a spend threshold (e.g., any single marketing spend over $300), brand pivots, pricing changes, or entering new channels require your approval. Specify the threshold clearly.

**6. Confidentiality.** Customer data, pricing strategy, product roadmap, telemetry data, and revenue figures are confidential during and after his involvement. No end date on confidentiality.

**7. Non-compete.** Standard clause preventing him from building or working on a directly competing product (local-first AI workspace) for 12-24 months after departure. Keep it narrow and geographically unconstrained to be enforceable.

**8. Buyback / repurchase right.** The company has the right (not obligation) to repurchase unvested shares at nominal price if he departs for any reason. This is your protection against a cap table with a passive ghost owner.

**9. Drag-along / tag-along.** If you sell Advisor Prep Hero, he cannot block the sale (drag-along protects you). If you sell your stake, he has the right to sell his proportionally (tag-along protects him). Both clauses are standard and non-controversial.

**10. Dispute resolution.** Specify mediation before litigation, and specify jurisdiction (your state). This matters especially for family: you want a structured process before anyone files a lawsuit.

**11. 83(b) election acknowledgment.** The agreement should explicitly note that he is responsible for filing an 83(b) election with the IRS within 30 days of the grant date and that you have advised him to consult a tax professional. Document that he received this advice.

### For a Revenue Share Agreement (if you choose this route instead)

**1. Revenue definition.** Is this gross revenue, net of payment processor fees, net of refunds, or something else? LemonSqueezy takes approximately 5% in fees. Be precise. "Revenue" means different things to different people.

**2. Payment schedule.** Monthly, quarterly, or upon receipt? Define the payment trigger and timeline explicitly (e.g., "within 15 business days of calendar month-end").

**3. Reporting transparency.** How does he verify the numbers? He should have read-only access to LemonSqueezy or Stripe. Opaque revenue reporting destroys trust faster than almost anything else.

**4. Term and termination.** How long does the agreement run? What notice period is required to terminate (suggest 30 days)? What happens to revenue share from sales that close in the pipeline at termination?

**5. Cap.** A revenue share with no cap can become a significant ongoing liability if the product scales significantly. Consider a cumulative cap (e.g., the revenue share ends once he has received $X total) or a time limit (e.g., "this agreement runs for 36 months then renegotiates").

**6. Performance minimums.** The agreement can include a minimum activity requirement. If he is not running at least X campaigns or delivering Y outputs per quarter, the agreement is reviewable by either party.

**7. IP and confidentiality.** Same as the equity agreement version above. IP created in the role belongs to the company.

**8. Non-compete.** Same as above.

## 8. One-Time Pricing Math: Why This Changes the Calculus

This is where Advisor Prep Hero's model creates a specific wrinkle that most co-founder guides do not address.

**Subscription SaaS revenue share math is predictable.** If a product has $5,000 MRR and grows, a 15% revenue share means $750/month growing over time. The partner can model their income. The founder can model their cost. Both are smooth and plannable.

**One-time pricing revenue share math is lumpy.** Advisor Prep Hero's revenue depends entirely on new customer acquisition. A Product Hunt launch week might generate $8,000 in 72 hours. The following six weeks might generate $400 total. A 15% revenue share means $1,200 at launch and $60 per week in quiet periods. This makes revenue share a poor standalone incentive for sustained marketing effort between spikes.

The implication is important: **pure revenue share is a worse structure for one-time pricing products than for subscription products**, and the overall structure should weight toward equity (deferred upside that compounds as the product grows) rather than immediate revenue share.

**If you do use revenue share, structure it with a floor:**
- Small monthly retainer ($200-400/month) for baseline activity
- Plus a commission percentage (8-12%) on sales above a threshold
- The retainer covers quiet periods; the commission rewards launches and spikes

**The equity vs. revenue share NPV comparison for Advisor Prep Hero specifically:**

Run these scenarios:

*Scenario A: 12% equity, no revenue share*
- Vests fully in year 4
- If Advisor Prep Hero sells for $300K in year 5: his share = $36,000
- If Advisor Prep Hero sells for $1M in year 5: his share = $120,000
- If Advisor Prep Hero sells for $3M in year 7: his share = $360,000

*Scenario B: No equity, 15% revenue share in perpetuity*
- Advisor Prep Hero generates $60K/year in average sales (modest growth)
- That is $9,000/year to him
- Over 4 years: $36,000 total with zero upside on any exit premium

The math strongly favors equity for any scenario where the company has meaningful exit value. Revenue share only "wins" for him if the company stays small and he exits the relationship early. This means if your brother genuinely believes in Advisor Prep Hero's trajectory, equity is the better deal for him too. Both of you benefit from the equity model in the long run.

**One-time pricing and revenue share caps:** If you do use revenue share, make it time-limited. A suggested structure: 10% of gross sales for months 1-24, declining to 5% for months 25-48, then converting to equity-only (assuming he is still active). This prevents revenue share from becoming a permanent lien on the business.

## 9. The Trial Period: Structure Before You Commit

Never skip this step. The trial period is what separates a good co-founder relationship from an expensive regret.

### The Recommended Structure

**Phase 1: 90-day paid engagement, no equity**

Before any equity agreement is signed, run a 90-day engagement where he delivers specific, measurable marketing outcomes for cash compensation.

Suggested deliverables for the 90-day period:
- A full Product Hunt launch plan (hunter shortlist, timing analysis, listing copy, 24-hour launch day playbook)
- 10 outreach touchpoints to newsletter operators, indie product directories, or community moderators (with documentation)
- A market research brief on 3 competitor positioning changes in the past 6 months
- At least one executed campaign (even a simple one: a Reddit post, a community thread, a cold email sequence)

Compensation: $500-800 total (not market rate, but a meaningful acknowledgment of real work). The purpose is not to pay him fairly for 3 months of full effort; it is to create a low-stakes structure where he can demonstrate capability and you can evaluate fit.

**Phase 2: Debrief at day 90**

A structured conversation covering:
- Did his work produce measurable results?
- Did he communicate well and proactively?
- Did he take initiative without needing direction on every task?
- Did he understand the product and the audience?
- Was the working relationship comfortable with real stakes involved?

If yes to all: proceed to formal equity agreement. If not: you part ways cleanly with no legal entanglement and no family financial dispute.

**Phase 3: Letter of engagement (for the trial period)**

Even the 90-day trial should have a simple 1-2 page letter of engagement specifying:
- This is a trial period; no equity is granted during this period
- IP created during this period belongs to Advisor Prep Hero
- Confidentiality applies from day one
- The trial can be ended by either party with 7 days' notice

This is not hostile. It is professional and protects both of you.

**Phase 4: Equity agreement**

If the trial succeeds, the equity agreement starts the clock from the date the formal agreement is signed, not from the trial start date. Trial months do not count toward vesting. This is clean and fair: the equity clock starts when the real commitment starts.

### Optional: Milestone Gate at the 6-Month Cliff

In addition to the time cliff, add one or two marketing milestones that must be achieved by the 6-month mark for the cliff vesting to trigger:
- "At least one launch campaign resulting in $X,000 in attributable sales" or
- "Completed market research brief and competitive positioning document, approved by founder"

This creates accountability without being adversarial. It defines what success looks like before it needs to be evaluated.

## 10. Resources, Frameworks, and Templates

### Books

**Slicing Pie** by Mike Moyer
The definitive text on dynamic equity splits for bootstrapped businesses. If you want a model that tracks contributions in real time rather than guessing a static split upfront, this is the framework. Available at slicingpie.com. The free sample PDF covers the core model.

**The Founder's Dilemmas** by Noam Wasserman
Research-backed analysis of how founding team decisions (equity splits, role assignments, family involvement) predict startup outcomes. Dense but concrete, and the family business section is directly relevant.

**Venture Deals** by Brad Feld
If Advisor Prep Hero ever goes toward investor capital, this is the definitive primer on term sheet mechanics. Not immediately needed, but worth reading before any investor conversation.

### Frameworks and Calculators

**Slicing Pie** (slicingpie.com)
Dynamic equity model where contributions are tracked as "slices" at fair market value with multipliers. Equity freezes at break-even. Eliminates most disputes because the formula is agreed upon before contributions are made.

**Gust Co-Founder Equity Calculator** (cofounders.gust.com)
Contribution-weighted scoring tool that outputs recommended splits. Useful for having a data-driven starting point for the conversation with your brother, rather than starting from "I think 10% sounds right."

**ICanPitch Split Framework** (icanpitch.com)
Scoring framework that weights idea origin, execution, capital contribution, and risk. Good for stress-testing a proposed split.

### Legal Templates

**Founder Institute FAST Agreement** (fi.co/fast)
The standard advisor agreement. Free, 1 page, widely recognized. Use this if you go the advisor route.

**Promise Legal Founder Agreement Template** (promise.legal)
Free template with vesting built in and legal commentary explaining each clause. Good starting draft for a full co-founder agreement.

**PandaDoc Founders Agreement** (pandadoc.com)
Free, customizable template covering all must-have clauses. Good for the initial draft before attorney review.

**Cooley GO** (cooleygo.com)
The best free legal resource library for early-stage founders. Covers 83(b) election guidance, founder stock basics, and template documents. Start here before choosing an attorney.

### Cap Table Management

**Carta** (carta.com)
The standard for equity management. Has a free tier for early-stage companies. Manages vesting schedules, cap tables, and 409A valuations. At Advisor Prep Hero's stage, the free tier is sufficient.

**Pulley** (pulley.com)
Carta alternative, more founder-friendly pricing at early stages. Worth comparing.

### Specific to One-Time Pricing

**Freemius Blog** (freemius.com/blog)
The definitive resource on lifetime deal mechanics and the economics of one-time pricing for desktop software products. Read their LTD guide before any major launch or revenue share discussion.

## 11. The Opinionated Recommendation

Given everything above, here is what the research points toward for Advisor Prep Hero specifically:

### Structure

**Hybrid: equity with a revenue share component.** Not pure equity, not pure revenue share.

### Equity

**10-12%, 4-year vest, 6-month cliff.**

This reflects:
- Your head start (product live, revenue flowing, all product risk taken by you)
- His part-time commitment (he presumably has his own job)
- The fact that this is a trusted long-term relationship deserving meaningful alignment
- Industry benchmarks for marketing co-founders joining post-launch

Below 10% risks creating a vendor dynamic. Above 15% undervalues the substantial product work already done.

### Revenue Share Component

**10% of gross sales (after LemonSqueezy/Stripe fees, before other expenses) for the first 24 months, then renegotiable.**

This gives him a near-term income signal, motivates him to drive launches, and is appropriate for a one-time pricing model because it aligns with sale spikes rather than creating false smoothness.

**Cap:** Total cumulative revenue share payout capped at $20K. After that, the revenue share sunsets and the relationship becomes equity-only. This prevents the revenue share from becoming a permanent drag on the business if it scales significantly.

### Trial Period

**90 days, paid ($600-800 total), no equity, specific deliverables.** This is non-negotiable. No exceptions for family.

### Entity and Legal

If Advisor Prep Hero is currently a sole prop or single-member LLC, convert to a multi-member LLC or Delaware C-Corp before issuing equity. Talk to a CPA and startup attorney before the equity agreement is signed. Budget $1,500-3,000 for this step. It is not optional.

### 83(b) Election

Both of you should consult a tax professional before the grant date. The 30-day filing window should be built into the agreement timeline as an explicit action item with a calendar reminder.

### The Agreement

Use the Promise Legal or PandaDoc template as a starting draft. Have a startup attorney (not a general practice attorney) review the final version. This should cost $500-1,500. It is not optional given family involvement.
