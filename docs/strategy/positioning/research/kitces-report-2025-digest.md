# Digest — The Kitces Report 2025, Vol. 1: "The Technology That Independent Financial Advisors Actually Use (And Like)"

**Source:** Kitces Research AdvisorTech Study, Volume 1, 2025 (209-page PDF).
**Raw files (this repo):** `docs/research/The-Kitces-Report-...-Vol-1-2025-1.pdf` (original) and `docs/research/kitces-report-2025-vol1.txt` (full text extraction, searchable).
**What this is:** The most authoritative independent survey of what software US independent financial advisors actually use and how satisfied they are. Skews independent RIAs / younger advisors. This digest captures the numbers and findings that matter for Keepance positioning. *Facts are neutral; a short "Relevance to Keepance" note is at the end.*

---

## 1. The state of AdvisorTech in 2025 (the big numbers)

- The **typical firm** (median: 5 team members, 2 lead advisors, 3 support staff, ~$1M revenue) runs **~12 distinct software applications** to support **~20 business functions** (out of 45 functions the report tracks).
- Tech spend ≈ **4% of revenue** for the median firm. The smallest solos spend a higher **6.2%** of revenue. Spending more does **not** buy more satisfaction — firms spending <1% report nearly the same satisfaction as those spending 8%+ (satisfaction peaks mildly at the 4–6% median).
- Even the **least** tech-sophisticated firms still run **~10 tools for ~16 functions** — software is now universal.
- **Overall tech-stack satisfaction fell to 7.0/10 in 2025, down from 7.3 in 2023.** Of 23 comparable categories, satisfaction **dropped in all but three**. Steepest drops were in business-development tools (websites, digital marketing, proposal generation).
- The report names the core problem: **"tech bloat" from "juggling too many individual tools."** Advisors are described as "an industry more reliant on technology than ever, yet increasingly dissatisfied with the solutions they feel forced into."

## 2. The stack & integration — the single most important finding for Keepance

- **Integration (data flowing automatically across apps) is the #1 driver of tech-stack satisfaction.**
- **Fewer than one-third** of advisors say data flows automatically across their main applications; only ~10% have fully integrated workflows beyond the core systems.
- Minimal-integration firms rate their stack **6.3/10**; fully-integrated firms rate it **7.9/10**. Average *integration* satisfaction is just **6.2** vs. 7.0 overall — integration is the weakest link.
- The five ranked drivers of stack satisfaction: **(1) Integration, (2) Incorporation (breadth of functions covered), (3) Autonomy (exclusive-RIA), (4) Nimbleness (smaller size), (5) Intentionality (proactive use).**
- **CRM is the hub** for ~48% of firms (55% at $1M–$3M firms); financial planning ~17%; portfolio management ~14%.
- Best-of-breed vs. all-in-one barely changes satisfaction (~38% Broad All-in-One, ~25% each Focused All-in-One / Focused Best-in-Class, 15% Broad Best-in-Class). **What matters is how well tools are integrated and used, not how many or which kind.**
- **Productivity reality check:** higher tech sophistication shows **no statistically detectable** revenue-per-advisor gain. The real productivity driver is **staff leverage** (support-staff-to-advisor ratio). "Technology can make work better, but real improvements in productivity come from people and process."
- **~8 in 10 advisors say technology's most important role is to deliver higher-quality advice / a better client experience — only ~2 in 10 say its job is firm efficiency.** (Important: advisors value client-outcome tech over back-office-efficiency tech, even though they adopt AI mostly for the back office — see §4.)

## 3. Switching & adoption behavior — why advisors resist new tools

- **89%** made ≥1 tech change in the last 12 months; **89%** plan ≥1 change in the next 12. So advisors *do* change — but selectively.
- **Only 14%** of switches are driven by dissatisfaction. **~48%** switch only when shown a solution they believe is **genuinely superior**. Switching costs (data migration, workflow change, retraining) are high; advisors default to **"the devil you know."**
- Key quote: *"The main obstacle to winning advisors away from their existing solutions is not simply proving that their current tools are inferior. It's overcoming inertia and the perceived switching costs of moving to a new solution."*
- **How advisors find/buy software (procurement inputs):** vendor demos **49%**, industry peers **43%**, blogs/podcasts **33%**, industry studies **31%**. Cold outbound, ads, and exhibit halls rank low. *"Don't call on us, we'll call on you when we've done our own research with our peers."*
- **Best green-field opportunities (high change-intent + high vendor uncertainty):** Agentic AI Assistants, Legacy Planning, Client Feedback, Outbound Prospecting.
- **Most entrenched (low switch-intent + low uncertainty):** eSignature, **client file sharing, document management,** scheduling, **CRM,** general financial planning. ← These are the categories Keepance is adjacent to; they are the *hardest* to displace, which is why Keepance must sit *over* them, not try to replace them.

## 4. Advisor attitudes toward AI (the core of the positioning question)

- Advisors are broadly, mildly optimistic about AI's 2-year productivity benefit (all five business objectives scored >5/10). Distribution: **~38% AI optimists, ~40% moderates, ~22% skeptics.**
- **They want a smart assistant, not autopilot:** **57%** expect AI to **expedite** key tasks (keep the human in the loop), **15%** want it to **help initiate** tasks (brainstorm), only **28%** want **full automation.** Report's takeaway: *"AI solutions should be more intentional about building 'human-in-the-loop' capabilities than highlighting AI as a hands-off automation solution."*
- **AI is wanted in the back office, not the client relationship.** Advisors are most optimistic about AI for operational efficiency, least optimistic about AI growing their client base. The **one** activity where a majority want AI to stay away is **client service** — *"advisors continue to view personalized, human-based service as a cornerstone of their value proposition... This does not bode well for AI chatbots as a client service solution."*
- **Current AI use by activity (fewer than 1 in 4 use AI for any single activity):** the **highest current use is reviewing client documents (20%)** — analyzing tax forms and estate documents via LLMs. (Caveat: much "document review" is OCR, not true AI; "true" AI adoption across core functions is likely **≤10% of advisors.**)
- **Interest is high where adoption is low:** >50% are interested in AI for **client data gathering, financial-plan preparation, and improving investment outcomes.** Most open to fully automating: **gathering client data.** Least willing to automate: **preparing the financial plan** (fiduciary-liability concern — they'll use AI to cut manual work but keep ownership of the advice).
- **The trust gap is the gating barrier:** *"AI tools still have a substantial trust gap to bridge before advisors feel confident relying on them to craft recommendations and deliver advice. After all, it sometimes takes only one big mistake for an advisor to lose a client — or face a career-ending lawsuit."*
- Framing quote advisors are hearing: *"Rather than AI replacing advisors, advisors using AI will replace those who don't."*

### The "Agentic AI Assistants" category (brand-new in 2025)
- **Importance score 7.3 (below median); adoption <10%** (lowest of the categories covered) — "early adopters only."
- Vendors named: **ChatGPT/general-purpose** (most common today), **Vega** (optimistic), **CogniCor** (neutral). CRMs and planning tools are starting to layer AI in (next-best-action, natural-language search).
- What advisors do with AI today: draft emails/marketing, summarize notes, rephrase client communications — **all back-office, none client-facing.**
- **Adoption barriers:** (1) **Compliance** — how is client data handled, is output archivable, how to avoid hallucinations; (2) **Trust/accuracy** — "a single mistake can lose a lifetime client or trigger a career-derailing lawsuit"; (3) **Integration** — "most implementations remain siloed"; (4) **Supervision cost** — "at the point that AI requires supervision, much of the time-savings is already lost."
- **Single-function AI tools (notetakers: Jump, Zocks) have far more adoption than autonomous agentic AI.** Solo advisors are ahead of enterprise firms (less red tape). Most agentic-AI interest "remains conceptual," waiting for peer-vetted, compliance-cleared, integrated offerings.

## 5. Vendor recognition (criteria + named leaders)
- **Market Leader** = 10%+ share + 8.0+ satisfaction. **Rising Star** = 8.2+ satisfaction, 1.3–10% share. **Standout** = 5%+ share + 8.5+ satisfaction.
- 2025 has **fewer Market Leaders** (only 9 categories have a 10%-share/8.0-satisfaction vendor, down from 15 in 2023) and a record **53 Rising Stars** across 21 categories — the market is fragmenting, not consolidating.

### Cross-category signals
- **Advyzon** = the report's most consistent rising star (top or near-top satisfaction in performance reporting, CRM, file sharing, email archiving, workflow) — but **bundled-only**.
- **Orion** = multi-year satisfaction decline across *every* product (all-in-one acquisition strategy diluting quality).
- **Microsoft (OneDrive/SharePoint)** = dominant in document management + file sharing on integration + familiarity, not advisor-specific features.
- **Jump** = the breakout AI success; **the only fully-validated, widely-adopted AI application in advisortech is AI meeting support.**

## Relevance to Keepance (interpretation — not from the report)

1. **The "another tool" objection is the market's dominant mood, with a name ("tech bloat") and a worsening trend (7.0, down from 7.3).** Any new tool must answer it explicitly.
2. **Integration is the #1 thing advisors reward; "siloed" is the #1 thing they (and the report) flag as the AI failure mode.** Keepance's "reads across the tools you already use" is pushing on the open door — *if it genuinely connects and isn't just another island.*
3. **Keepance's lane is validated:** advisors want AI in the **back office** (prep, document review, drafting), with a **human in the loop** (57% expedite vs 28% automate), and explicitly **not** in client service. The single most-used real AI task today — **reading client documents (20%)** — is Keepance's core capability.
4. **The trust gap ("one mistake = a lawsuit") is the gating barrier.** Keepance's **verifiable citations** + **local-first/BYOK data control** are direct answers to the two named blockers (accuracy/trust + compliance/data-handling).
5. **The trap to avoid:** being filed under "Agentic AI Assistants" — low importance, <10% adoption, distrusted. Keepance should **not** lead as "another AI assistant"; it should lead as the **integration + answer layer over the existing stack**, with the prep/drafting workflow as the hero use case.
6. **Go-to-market implication:** advisors buy via **demos + peers + industry research**, only for a **clearly superior** product, and resist switching. Keepance should *avoid asking them to switch away from anything* (it sits on top), and win through a demo-able "wow" + warm peer referrals (e.g., the design-partner advisor).
