## **Part I — Legal Ethics and Professional Responsibility**

### **I-A. Competence and Technology (ABA Model Rule 1.1)**

**Q1.** Technological competence under ABA Model Rule 1.1 affirmatively requires a continuous, reasonable understanding of the specific capabilities and inherent limitations of any generative artificial intelligence tool employed in a legal practice. The standard, heavily clarified by ABA Formal Opinion 512, rejects the notion of a static understanding.1 Competence demands a hybrid comprehension: the practitioner must understand at a high level how the tool processes data (specifically, that it functions as a probabilistic pattern-matching engine rather than a deterministic legal database) and must possess the substantive legal knowledge to critically evaluate the output.3 The standard dictates that attorneys account for reliability, accuracy, completeness, and bias.2 Because the system generates statistically likely text that may manifest as entirely fabricated case law—a phenomenon known as a hallucination—competence requires rigorous, independent verification of all generated citations, factual assertions, and legal analyses against primary source materials.4 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q2.** The requirement to affirmatively disclose AI usage to a client is highly contextual and not universally mandated for all routine tasks. ABA Formal Opinion 512 and corresponding state bar guidelines indicate that disclosure under Model Rule 1.4 is triggered when the application of AI materially alters the risk profile of the representation, specifically regarding the exposure of confidential information to third-party models, or when the client explicitly expects independent human drafting.6 When disclosure is necessary, the required specificity generally pertains to the operational fact that AI is being utilized and the procedural safeguards protecting the client's data, rather than the intricate backend data architecture of the specific provider.7 However, jurisdictional divergence is actively developing. For instance, New York guidelines emphasize the fundamental duty to protect data over mandatory disclosure, whereas other advisory bodies suggest preemptively incorporating broad AI usage disclosures into initial engagement letters to maintain transparency and forestall ethical challenges.9 *Confidence Level:* *Resolution:* A comprehensive 50-state survey of newly issued ethics opinions mapping the exact threshold defining "routine" versus "substantive" AI assistance is required to determine the exact triggers for mandatory Rule 1.4 communications in target jurisdictions.  
**Q3.** The utilization of Keepance, assuming the AI output is systematically reviewed and substantially edited by the attorney prior to utilization, definitively constitutes competent use under the current state of professional opinion. The fundamental distinction between competent and incompetent AI adoption lies in the execution of independent professional judgment.1 Disciplinary tribunals have consistently sanctioned attorneys not for the mere use of AI, but for the blind reliance on its unverified outputs.4 Competent drafting requires treating the generated text as an unverified preliminary draft. The attorney must actively intervene, verify all legal propositions, correct structural deficiencies, and assume ultimate professional responsibility for the final document as if they had drafted it entirely from inception.7 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*

### **I-B. Confidentiality (ABA Model Rule 1.6)**

**Q4.** Transmitting client information through a cloud AI provider's API is legally analogous to utilizing modern cloud computing or electronic discovery vendors, provided it falls within the "reasonable efforts" safe harbor of Rule 1.6(c). ABA Formal Opinion 512, alongside state bar advisories, permits the integration of third-party technologies if the attorney executes adequate due diligence regarding data security.1 The operative distinction rests on the contractual data confidentiality provisions. Enterprise API agreements that explicitly mandate zero-retention policies—legally guaranteeing that data is neither retained on vendor servers nor utilized for the training of underlying foundation models—provide a robust defense against characterization as an unethical disclosure.14 Conversely, transmitting data through consumer-facing interfaces governed by standard terms of service that allow model training constitutes an immediate breach of Rule 1.6.9 *Confidence Level:* *Resolution:* A precise legal audit of the enterprise API Service Level Agreements (SLAs) for Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google is required to confirm the absolute absence of data retention loopholes or secondary data-sharing clauses with affiliates.  
**Q5.** A local-first architecture significantly strengthens the Rule 1.6 compliance posture by fundamentally reducing the application's attack surface. By eliminating the necessity of an intermediate software-as-a-service vendor server and routing the data strictly from the user's local machine to the target AI provider's API endpoint, the architecture mathematically decreases the points of potential interception or breach.17 This design structurally aligns with the "reasonable efforts" mandate of Rule 1.6 by eliminating unauthorized secondary storage and removing the software vendor from the data chain of custody entirely.1 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*

### **I-C. Attorney-Client Privilege and Work Product Doctrine**

**Q8.** The transmission of privileged communications to a cloud AI provider's API does not inherently trigger a waiver of the attorney-client privilege if the AI provider functions analogously to a secure, necessary clerical or technology vendor. Established privilege jurisprudence, supported by recent case law, maintains that disclosures to third-party agents essential for the provision of modern legal services do not destroy privilege, provided there exists a reasonable expectation of confidentiality. An enterprise API governed by strict non-disclosure, zero-retention agreements structurally satisfies this expectation. This environment is fundamentally distinct from consumer-facing AI models, where permissive privacy policies legally dismantle the expectation of confidentiality necessary to maintain privilege.15 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q9.** The waiver analysis fundamentally shifts based on the identified architectural and procedural variables. Contractual data confidentiality protections (a) are the definitive legal factor preventing waiver, as they formally enforce the expectation of privacy and establish the provider as an agent rather than a true third party.15 Processing data strictly on the user's machine (b) eliminates external transmission entirely, nullifying the waiver risk at the infrastructure level.17 Affirmative steps to exclude or anonymize privileged content from AI prompts (c) completely bypass the privilege issue, rendering the transmission legally benign regardless of the provider's terms of service, as no privileged communication is ever actually transmitted.9 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q10.** The case *United States v. Heppner*, No. 25 Cr. 503 (JSR), 2026 BL 52143 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026), exists and represents a critical ruling on AI and privilege. Decided by Judge Jed S. Rakoff, the court held that a criminal defendant's written exchanges with a publicly available generative AI platform (Claude) regarding legal strategy were not protected by either the attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine.20 The court's holding rested on three pillars: first, the AI tool is not an attorney, negating the required professional relationship; second, communications on a public AI platform lack a reasonable expectation of confidentiality due to the provider's privacy policy allowing data collection for model training; and third, the defendant interacted with the tool on his own initiative, not at the direction of counsel to facilitate legal advice.15 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q11.** The work product doctrine, designed to protect an attorney's mental impressions, legal theories, and litigation strategy, applies distinctly from attorney-client privilege. While privilege is fragile and easily waived by unprivileged disclosure, work product protection is generally waived only if a disclosure substantially increases the likelihood that an adversary will obtain the information.19 Transmitting work product through a secure, enterprise cloud AI API governed by strict confidentiality protections does not increase adversary access, thereby preserving the protection. Conversely, inputting strategic work product into a public AI tool that trains on user data risks waiving the doctrine entirely.19 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q12.** A local AI conversation log storing procedural metadata—such as timestamps, the specific model utilized, and the output filename—is highly likely to be discoverable in litigation as Electronically Stored Information (ESI) under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 34\. The metadata itself is procedural and operational, not substantive, and thus unprivileged.10 However, if the full log contains the attorney's specific prompts that reflect legal strategy, factual summaries, or mental impressions, that substantive content is fiercely protected under the work product doctrine. In a discovery context, the existence of the log would be acknowledged, but the substantive prompt data would be withheld and cataloged on a privilege log.6 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*

Blind reliance on an AI output that generates a hallucinated or mischaracterized contradiction will lead directly to sanctions, as the attorney failed to exercise independent verification before presenting the fact to a tribunal or opposing counsel.4 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*

### **I-G. Patent-Specific Issues (EU Absolute-Novelty Rule)**

## **Part II — Tax and Accounting Regulation**

### **II-A. IRC §7216 — Scope and Application**

**Q23.** The current product framing characterizing the §7216 risk as merely an "open question" severely understates the statutory reality. Under Treasury Regulation § 301.7216-1(b)(5), "disclosure" is expansively defined as the act of making tax return information known to any person in any manner whatever.35 An API call transmitting client return data from a local machine to an external commercial provider (such as Anthropic or OpenAI) undeniably constitutes a statutory disclosure. The actual unresolved regulatory question is not whether a disclosure occurs, but whether the AI API provider qualifies under the "auxiliary services" exception outlined in Regs. Sec. 301.7216-1(b)(2)(iii), which permits disclosures to third parties providing specific supporting services to the tax preparer without explicit taxpayer consent.35 Without a definitive Internal Revenue Service Private Letter Ruling classifying LLM API providers as auxiliary services, affirmative consent remains technically and legally required. *Confidence Level:* *Resolution:* Formal IRS guidance or an authoritative Private Letter Ruling specifically classifying enterprise AI API providers as "auxiliary services" under Treas. Reg. § 301.7216-1(b)(2)(iii) is required to definitively resolve the consent mandate.  
**Q24.** Treasury Regulation § 301.7216-3 mandates exact precision in the execution of consent forms, strictly requiring the identification of the specific recipient of the disclosure.36 Describing the recipient as a broad, ambiguous category (e.g., "an AI language model provider") is legally insufficient and invalidates the entire consent document. To manage multi-provider uncertainty effectively, the §7216 consent form must explicitly list all potential AI providers by their exact corporate names (e.g., "Anthropic, OpenAI, and/or Google") and stipulate the precise data to be shared with them.36 Blanket consents authorizing undefined future disclosures are strictly prohibited under the regulations. *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q25.** The scope of IRC §7216 is uniquely and intentionally expansive. "Tax return information" encompasses any information furnished for, or in connection with, the preparation of a tax return. This statutory definition explicitly extends to: (a) Documents the client provides that are not technically tax data, such as a lease agreement or corporate minutes, provided to analyze deductibility. (b) Preparer-generated analysis, derivative information, algorithms, and statistical compilations that incorporate or are based upon return data. (c) Information gathered during a client interview or via a tax organizer that has not yet been, or may never be, committed to a finalized return.36 *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q26.** Characterizing an intentional §7216 violation as a "federal criminal misdemeanor" is technically and statutorily accurate; the statute imposes severe criminal penalties of up to a $1,000 fine and one year in prison for knowing or reckless unauthorized disclosures.35 However, emphasizing the criminal aspect obscures the more immediate and realistic enforcement path. IRC §6713 imposes civil penalties of $250 per unauthorized disclosure, up to a maximum of $100,000 annually, without requiring the government to prove knowing or reckless intent.35 The civil penalty mechanism, followed closely by professional licensing revocation actions by the Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR), is the primary enforcement vector. *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*  
**Q27.** The IRC §7216 statutory analysis applies uniformly to all tax return preparers, whether they are non-attorney CPAs, Enrolled Agents, or licensed tax attorneys. While attorney-client privilege or the IRC §7525 tax practitioner privilege protects specific legal communications from forced disclosure to the government or third parties during adversarial litigation, these privileges do not bypass, override, or negate the affirmative statutory prohibitions of §7216 regarding the unauthorized, commercial disclosure of data to third-party technology providers.36 Attorney-preparers must comply strictly with the §7216 written consent requirements in addition to maintaining their Rule 1.6 ethical duties. *Confidence Level: \[HIGH\]*
